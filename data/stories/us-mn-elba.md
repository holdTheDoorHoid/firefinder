The Elba fire tower stands on a high bluff just east of the town of Elba, in southeastern Minnesota.[^2] The Department of Natural Resources places it about two miles from Whitewater State Park, while the Register says it is immediately adjacent to the park.[^3][^1] A 1936 newspaper report, quoted on the Central US Lookouts site, called it the first forest service lookout tower in southeastern Minnesota and said it would be manned in dry weather to protect the Whitewater area.[^2] The Register says the Civilian Conservation Corps built it in 1936.[^1] The DNR gives 1933 as the build year, and so does a 1997 newspaper article, which adds that the tower was built to stamp out man-made fires.[^3][^4] The sources disagree on the build date.[^1][^3]

The Register gives the tower as 110 feet tall, with a 7-by-7-foot cab, and the 1936 report gives the same height.[^1][^2] A 1973 survey note describes steel construction with a wooden platform and crow's nest, and puts the height at about 130 feet.[^2] A 1997 newspaper article says the tower is 100 feet tall.[^4] The height is not settled.

In 1959 the tower was manned when conditions indicated a fire hazard.[^2] Newspapers name George Meyers, superintendent of the Whitewater Game Refuge, as the man who manned it in 1952, and Peter Kronebusch as towerman in 1959.[^2] State reports for 1935 and 1936 say no fire needed the tower in either season.[^2] The Register says the state forestry division no longer mans it.[^1]

Fewer fires and better land care meant the tower was no longer needed, so it was abandoned.[^4] Its steps rotted, the DNR closed it, and it stood empty for more than 30 years.[^4] A 1997 article says it was retired in the early 1960s.[^4] The Register says the state parks division took the tower over for restoration as an interpretive site.[^1] A Post-Bulletin article dated June 26, 1997 announced that the restored tower would reopen to visitors after a Saturday dedication.[^4]

Today the DNR lists climbing the tower as a nearby activity.[^3] It is open during daylight hours from April through October, and the agency says it is used for recreation and education.[^3] Reaching the base means about 500 uneven steps, according to the DNR.[^3] The 1997 article counted 634 steps from the parking lot off Winona 26 to the base, and gave opening hours of 5 a.m. to 10 p.m. daily.[^4] The DNR page is undated, so check the current hours and the stairs before you go.[^3] The 1997 article also places the tower on DNR wildlife land.[^4]
