Soda Mountain Lookout stands on the Siskiyou Range in southern Oregon, four miles north of the California border and about 13 miles southeast of Ashland.[^1][^2] A crew of Civilian Conservation Corps workers finished the first cabin in 1933, an L-4 cab on a short tower.[^1][^3] A 1934 field report said Soda Mountain would cover the east side of Jenny Creek, the part of the mountain that was otherwise out of view.[^3]

In 1961 and 1962 crews raised the cabin and built a new tower.[^3] The present cab, a 14-by-14-foot flat-roofed Oregon Department of Forestry cab on a 10-foot wooden tower, dates from 1962.[^1][^2] The sources disagree on the height: a 1993 entry says a four-foot raise produced a 9-foot-10-inch tower.[^3]

The Oregon Department of Forestry operated the lookout, according to BLM staff who spoke with a 2023 visitor.[^4] A January 1935 notice named Ira Tungate as the lookout the previous summer, and by 1952 Thelma Sims was stationed on the summit.[^3] In August 1955 lightning struck the station and knocked out its telephone system, the only damage reported.[^3] The same account noted that Sims was in her sixth or seventh year on the mountain.[^3]

Communications gear came to the summit in 1968.[^3] In 1992 Cellular One, a telephone company, installed a tower and equipment building beside the cab, and the view from the lookout was blocked.[^3]

Firelookout.com says the cab was staffed every summer through 2017, and a visitor found it staffed in July 2017.[^2][^5] A May 2023 visit found the cab closed up with a camera for remote monitoring, and BLM staff confirmed it was no longer staffed.[^5][^4] The National Historic Lookout Register entry still describes the lookout as active, which the 2023 visit does not support.[^1][^4]

The site is BLM land inside the Cascade-Siskiyou National Monument, near the Pacific Crest Trail.[^1] The lookout was registered on the National Historic Lookout Register on June 15, 2000.[^1]

Reaching the summit takes a hike. A 2017 visitor parked at the Hobart Bluff trailhead, walked south on the Pacific Crest Trail for about a mile, then took a cutoff and a rough dirt road just under a mile to the top.[^5] Sources describe the cab as closed and the summit as crowded with communications equipment, so the cab should be treated as off limits rather than a place to climb.[^4]
