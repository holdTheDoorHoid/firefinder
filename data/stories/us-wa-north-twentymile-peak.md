North Twentymile Peak has carried a fire lookout in some form since around 1920, when the Forest Service put up a simple log cabin on the Okanogan National Forest summit.[^1] One of the earliest lookouts there was Gwendolyn Creveling, later known as Gwennie Yockey, one of the few women working as a fire lookout in the country at the time; a newspaper report from the 1920s named her as the lookout on "North 20-Mile mountain in the Methow."[^2]

In 1923 the agency replaced the cabin with a D-6 cupola-style lookout house, a design that's now rare — only nine of the more than 200 built in the Northwest are said to survive.[^1] That cupola building is still standing on the summit today, though it's used for storage rather than as a working lookout.[^1][^2] Lookout Carl Albin worked from the station in the late 1920s, and other named observers over the following decades included Paul Heaton in 1952 and Bob Crandall in the mid-1950s.[^3]

In 1947 the Forest Service added a 30-foot timber tower topped with a 14-by-14-foot hip-roofed L-4 cab and catwalk, which still stands beside the older cupola.[^1][^2] The lookout was staffed into the late 1980s, with 1988 given as the last season of regular staffing.[^1][^2]

Since then it has been kept as a standby or "emergency" lookout rather than rented out or regularly staffed.[^1][^2] Volunteers have worked with the Methow Valley Ranger District to maintain both towers; a 2021 work party planned to repair and repaint them had to be called off when the Cub Creek 2 Fire closed access to the area two days beforehand, and the towers were later wrapped for fire protection as the fire spread north.[^2]

Reaching the lookout today means a demanding, largely unshaded hike of about 4,500 feet of elevation gain to just over 7,000 feet, on a trail that can be faint in spots and prone to blowdown from past wildfire damage.[^2] There's little reliable water along the route, so hikers need to carry their own.[^2]
