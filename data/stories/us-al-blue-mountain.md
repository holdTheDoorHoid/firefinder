Anniston's fire tower on Blue Mountain was one of the watch towers the state forest service ran in Calhoun County. Its job was to spot forest fires quickly and have them put out before they could do thousands of dollars' worth of damage to timber.[^1] The Anniston Land Company donated the tower site and planned to give the right of way up the mountain, and the city and county collaborated in getting a truck trail built so materials could be hauled to the top.[^1] A July 1942 report planned an 80-foot (24 m) tower.[^1] A November 1942 report said the finished tower would rise 54 feet (16 m) above the peak, so the two figures disagree, and the source does not say which one was built.[^1]

By May 1943 the concrete and steel foundations were in place in the northeast part of the city, and the creosoted timbers were ready for assembly.[^1] An Anniston Star item that May said the tower would set Anniston apart, since no other municipality in the nation would have one.[^1]

Trouble came early. A June 1944 lightning strike smashed every window and damaged the frame, and the sheriff put the repairs at about $75.[^1] By 1949 teenagers and others had shot out 44 of the 48 window panes with rifles, and the ranger warned that officers would act if it kept up.[^1]

Money was short too. In 1953 a shortage of funds threatened to remove the area's fire patrolman and close the tower. The ranger suggested the county contribute $2,000, with the state matching any local money.[^1] In October 1954 vandals broke in, wrecked the equipment and knocked out window frames, causing about $100 of damage. The ranger thought the state would move the tower elsewhere, and by then it was not manned on a regular basis.[^1]

In February 1956 a plan for six posts in the county included the Blue Mountain tower, and in October 1956 the Star reported that Fred Troiano would man it.[^1]

The last dated item on the Forest Lookouts page is from October 1956.[^1] None of the sources consulted says whether the tower stayed on the summit, was moved, or was taken down. No source describes what remains on Blue Mountain today or whether the public can reach the summit.[^1]
