Thompson Peak Lookout stands on a summit above the town of Janesville in Plumas County, with views over Honey Lake Valley.[^1][^3] The register says it was constructed and opened in June 1931.[^1] A Summit Post write-up says the Civilian Conservation Corps began the work in 1931 and that the two-story lookout was completed in 1932.[^3] A July 1931 newspaper item says wind at the summit gave the carpenters trouble and that the station needed heavy cables to stay in place.[^2]

The register places the design in the R1 class, a Region 1 modification of a California Region 4AR plan.[^1] Harry Foster was at the lookout station by September 1931.[^2] In October 1945 the forest ended operations at all but one of its 19 lookouts, and Thompson Peak was the one still watching the Honey Lake slope.[^2]

In August 1933 a fire that began beneath a logging truck burned close to 600 acres south of the peak. The lookout reported it, and two hundred men from a CCC camp were called in to fight it.[^2]

After World War II the Air Force identified the summit as a site for a gap-filler radar.[^3] Summit Post says a 1958 land agreement preceded construction, that the radar was working by 1960 and ran until 1967, and that the land returned to the Forest Service in 1969.[^3] The register gives 1958 to 1970 for the radar facility, while a Peakbagging caption gives 1960 to 1967, so the end date is disputed.[^1][^4] An abandoned building from that era still sits below the lookout.[^4]

In June 1991 the Forest Service reported that the lookout tower had been vandalized, and no damage estimate was available.[^2] In August and September 2016 the lookout and its access road were closed for construction, and fire watch coverage was not expected to be affected.[^2] The register added the lookout on October 26, 2019, as US 1340.[^1]

Today the Plumas National Forest manages the lookout.[^1] Summit Post says it was seasonally staffed as of 2012, and Peakbagging says it is usually staffed during fire season.[^3][^4] A seasonal four-wheel-drive road, NFS Road 28N02A, climbs the mountain's southeast slopes.[^3] No source consulted confirms staffing or road conditions after 2012, so check with the Plumas National Forest before you go.
