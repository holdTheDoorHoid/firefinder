Horse Butte Lookout stands on a knob on a peninsula that juts into Hebgen Lake, about seven miles northwest of West Yellowstone in Gallatin County.[^2][^4] The Gallatin National Forest looks after the tower.[^2][^5]

A crow's nest came first, though sources disagree on when.[^1][^2][^3][^5] The National Historic Lookout Register says a platform went up in the 1920s.[^1] Montana Lookouts puts a crow's nest in a tree around 1930, and firelookout.com dates one to about 1930.[^3][^2] A Gallatin National Forest fire manager, quoted by the Bozeman Daily Chronicle, said a crow's nest platform went up in a Douglas fir at the tower's current location in the late 1920s.[^5] The current tower replaced that crow's nest in 1953.[^5]

The live-in L-4 cab measures 14 by 14 feet and sits on a 41-foot treated timber tower.[^1][^2] Montana Lookouts and the Chronicle give the height as 40 feet.[^3][^4]

Staffing is where the sources diverge. An October 2012 visit report on the register says the tower is staffed only sometimes, is used in emergencies, and that the district has a four-year plan to get the tower back to being a staffed lookout.[^1] A Chronicle feature whose date does not appear in the copy we read describes a lookout who manned the tower six days a week in fire season, and calls Horse Butte the only one of the forest's three fire towers that was regularly manned.[^4] A Chronicle article about a smokejumper project quotes a fire manager saying the tower is staffed during high fire danger, with an emergency firefighter brought in when lightning is heavy.[^5]

In 2012 a volunteer crew removed wire fencing, reinforced the steps, treated the timbers, and added side rails and new windows.[^5] In a separate project, retired smokejumpers repainted the tower and built eight new shutters.[^5]

The tower is still standing, and the Chronicle describes it as solid but in need of steady upkeep.[^4] The register lists it as US 737 and records its registration on June 18, 2008.[^1] The 2012 visit report also notes a Forest Service repeater in the tower.[^1] Firelookout.com describes the tower as available during emergencies.[^2] None of the sources reviewed says the public may climb it, so ask the Forest Service before planning a visit.

The sources also disagree on the summit's elevation. The register gives 6,872 feet, while firelookout.com and the Chronicle give 7,077 feet.[^1][^2][^5]
