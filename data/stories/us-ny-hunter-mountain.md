Hunter Mountain has carried a fire-watch post longer than almost anywhere else in the Catskills. New York's Forest, Fish and Game Commission set up the first station there in 1909, built from timbers found right on the mountaintop, about 40 feet below the true summit.[^1] It was one of the earliest fire-detection stations the state ever ran, part of a program the commission launched after severe drought fires in 1903 and 1908 pushed the state to copy Maine's network of observation towers.[^2]

The wooden tower didn't last. In 1917 the state put up the steel Aermotor LS-40 tower that still stands today, 60 feet tall, going up beside where the wood tower had been.[^1] For a time it stayed put at that same 4,000-foot level; only in 1950 did crews take it apart and reassemble it where it sits now, on the mountain's actual high point at 4,040 feet.[^1] That history makes it the earliest of 23 fire towers New York State would go on to build in the region, and today it holds the record as the tallest-elevation fire tower left standing in the state.[^2]

The tower fell into disrepair by the 1990s, badly enough that the DEC, which had run it, recommended tearing it down.[^2] Instead, it earned a spot on the National Register of Historic Places in 1997, and a push by local supporters, with the DEC putting up matching funds, paid to bring both the tower and its observer's cabin back into shape.[^2] In 2017 the tower turned 100, marked with a plaque honoring its observers and attended by the last known surviving one.[^1]

Today Hunter Mountain Fire Tower works as a volunteer-run museum piece rather than an active fire lookout. Since 1996 it's been part of the Catskill Fire Tower Project, a partnership between the DEC, conservation groups, and local fire-tower committees, and volunteers staff the cab on weekends from Memorial Day through Columbus Day.[^1] It's reached by marked DEC hiking trails shared with horses, with water and shelter about a mile below the summit.[^1]
