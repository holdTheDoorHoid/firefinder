Bertha Hill stands in Clearwater County, Idaho, and the register lists the tower as belonging to the Clearwater-Potlatch Timber Protective Association.[^1] The register gives the elevation as 5,477 feet, while Rex Kamstra's page gives 5,520.[^1][^2]

The summit was first called Thunder Mountain.[^2] Kamstra's page says the present name most likely came from college students at a trail camp, though it notes that various interpretations exist.[^2]

The register dates the first post at the site to 1900.[^1] A 1957 newspaper, quoted on Forest Lookouts, puts the founding at 1900 and the construction at 1902.[^1][^4] Kamstra's page calls Mable Gray, the cook at the trail camp, the first lookout here.[^2] Idaho Fire Lookouts says that in 1902 she went up a tree perch two times daily to scan for smoke.[^3]

Later towers followed one after another. A pole platform went up in 1906, a 20-foot pole tower with a small enclosed cab in 1918, and in 1933 a 40-foot pole tower with an L-4 cab, built by Civilian Conservation Corps crews.[^1][^3][^4] A 1955 association report, quoted on Forest Lookouts, dates that L-4 tower to 1934.[^4] Idaho Fire Lookouts gives 1923 for another 20-foot tower with a small glass cabin, so the early dates do not fully agree.[^3]

The current tower is a steel structure, which the register gives as 56 feet tall with a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab.[^1] Its build date is disputed: the register says 1959, and Kamstra's page and Idaho Fire Lookouts say 1958.[^1][^2][^3] The association's annual reports, quoted on Forest Lookouts, describe work starting in 1955 and the tower completed in 1957.[^4] Kamstra's page adds that its superstructure came from Scofield Lookout.[^2]

Kamstra's page says two lookouts died on duty here.[^2] The register gives 1931 for a lightning death on the earlier pole tower, while Idaho Fire Lookouts gives 1932.[^1][^3] The other death came after a fall from the steel tower. Newspaper and association records put it in July 1964, and the association calls it accidental, while the register gives 1966.[^1][^4] Idaho Fire Lookouts says a metal skirt went on the tower afterward as a safeguard.[^3]

Regular staffing ran through the 1995 fire season, and Kamstra's page says the tower has served as an emergency detection site since.[^2] Idaho Fire Lookouts, writing in 2021, says the tower remains in use and calls it the longest continuously staffed one.[^3] The register added it on October 8, 1991.[^1]

The register notes a public observation platform at 46 feet.[^1] A 1958 association report says visitors could reach the observatory platform but not go beyond it.[^4] No current visiting rules turned up in the sources read, and the tower remains a drive-up emergency stand-by site, so anyone hoping to visit should check with the association first.[^3]
