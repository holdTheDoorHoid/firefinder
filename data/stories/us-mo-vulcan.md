Missouri's Conservation Commission bought about 128 acres for a fire tower in 1939, mainly to prevent wildfires.[^1][^2] More land was bought in the 1940s.[^2] The site is now part of the Riverside Conservation Area, west of the community of Vulcan in Iron County.[^2]

The first tower here was a temporary structure, and the sources differ on its type and dates. An annual-report summary lists a temporary pole tower for 1939 to 1941, then a 52-foot (16 m) wooden tower erected in 1943.[^1] The report instead says a temporary wood tower went up in 1943 and was dismantled in 1954.[^2]

The steel tower on the site was bought as a kit and erected in 1955 and 1956, according to the report, and the manufacturer was the Aermotor Company.[^2] It is 60 feet (18 m) tall, with a galvanized steel frame, four concrete footings and a pyramid roof over the cab.[^2] The report says it resembles Aermotor's LS-40 or MC-39 designs.[^2] An annual report for 1955 to 1956 describes it as the replacement for the wooden tower.[^1] The same report notes that topographic maps show a tower on the site by 1954, so the exact date is still open.[^2]

The site also had a tower residence.[^1] A tower man's house, garage, barn and chicken shed went up in the 1940s, and the report dates the house to 1949.[^2] State annual reports list repairs and remodeling to the tower residence from 1968 to 1982.[^1]

In the 1980s the Missouri Department of Conservation planned to remove the tower and all its buildings, but local support saved the tower.[^2] The other buildings came down in 1989.[^2] In 2013 a radio repeater went onto the tower, and it took on a new job as a receiving site for emergency radio traffic in southern Iron County.[^1][^2]

In an infrastructure assessment of MDC areas between 2018 and 2019, the forester recommended removing the tower because of continued vandalism, and an electrical check in early 2019 found the system did not meet state or federal code.[^2] The report recommends the tower as eligible for the National Register of Historic Places, citing its architectural significance and its link to state forestry history.[^2] The same report says the tower is slated for demolition, and it recommends documenting the structure to state and federal standards first.[^2]

A chain-link fence topped with barbed wire surrounds the base.[^2] The report says the fence is meant to deter climbing and vandalism.[^2] The site is reached by a tower access road off Highway BB.[^2] No source I could read describes what remains at the site today.
