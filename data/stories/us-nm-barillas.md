Barillas Peak has carried a lookout since before 1920, when the earliest tower on the summit was an old Weather Bureau tower.[^2] The Forest completed a cabin there in 1926, and by 1928 a small enclosed metal cab sat on a 30-foot tower.[^2] The National Historic Lookout Register instead describes the first lookout as a 7-by-7-foot cab built in the 1930s, which does not match the dates and size in Zinn's history.[^1][^2]

The tower standing today is 54 feet tall and carries a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab.[^1] It was completed in November 1959.[^2] The Register and a 2020 Forest Fire Lookout Association report both call it an Aermotor.[^1][^3]

Esther Harbison, whose husband was a ranger, staffed the lookout in 1920 and 1921, and Zinn knows of no other woman who worked a Forest lookout before World War II.[^2] Tony Vigil was the first person to work the 1959 tower.[^2] Gail Roos and Rich Rydin were the lookouts in 1974.[^2] After the late 1970s staffing became patchy, and Richard Martinez worked the summers of 1982 and 1983.[^2] The Register says the lookout is not staffed, though it gives no date for that.[^1]

A fire that started at a sawmill below the peak in April 1925 swept over the summit, but the tower appears to have survived.[^2] In June 1950 the Commissary Fire started below the abandoned lookout.[^2] A review board said a guard on the peak might have held it down, and the lookout was reopened as an emergency station.[^2] The Cat and Dog Fire of 1971 forced the lookout's evacuation.[^2]

The 1959 tower replaced an older one.[^2] Zinn says the cab left on the ground was scrapped after 1971 and later became a playhouse in East Pecos.[^2] In the same chapter she places the original cab on private land west of the peak, so the two accounts do not match.[^2] The old cabin was demolished in the late 1960s.[^2] The Register listed the tower on December 7, 2007.[^1][^2] In 1988 a thematic National Register review found the lookout not eligible because it was too young, and the 2020 report says it should be reviewed again.[^3]

The Forest Fire Lookout Association lists the tower as standing, and the Register rates its condition as good.[^4][^1] The sources read do not say whether the public may drive to the summit or climb the tower. A road reached the peak by 1958, but that is history, not a current rule.[^2] Check with the Santa Fe National Forest's Pecos/Las Vegas Ranger District before you go, and do not climb a tower that is closed or posted.[^3]
