Skookum Butte Lookout is a small square cabin of log and frame, built in 1928 on a boulder outcrop within yards of the Montana-Idaho border.[^1][^3] The register says the lookout is one of only three remaining L-5 log-and-frame ground houses in Montana and northern Idaho. A 2019 newspaper report says two of those three are on the Lolo National Forest.[^1][^3] The builders used mortise-and-tenon joints, which the newspaper calls unusual techniques. Each horizontal log has a peg on its end that fits into a groove along a standing log.[^3]

It sits more than eight miles up Elk Meadows Road, west of Lolo, Montana.[^3] The sources disagree on its height. The register gives 6,772 feet (2,064 meters), while the 2019 report and a lookout-history site both give 7,215 feet.[^1][^2][^3] The history site also places it in Idaho County, Idaho, where the register puts it in Missoula County, Montana.[^1][^2]

The register notes that it has not been regularly staffed since sometime before 1965. The history site says it has not been staffed since the early 1960s.[^1][^2] The National Historic Lookout Register listed it on June 5, 1996.[^1]

By 2011 the building was in poor shape. A visit report dated September 4, 2011 describes a more pronounced lean to the south, roughly half the shutters gone, and rot at the bottom of the cab's logs.[^1]

The push to save it got its green light in 2016.[^3] In 2019, historic preservation specialist Jack Poppen reset the stones under the southeast corner, and 11 volunteers from the Passport In Time program joined Forest Service staff to finish a first phase of repairs.[^3][^2] The work focused on the south and west sides, which suffer the most weather damage.[^3] The Forest Service has money for such work through its Heritage Stewardship Enhancement Program, and the project secured $20,000 for supplies and hauling 22 logs to the site.[^3] Many volunteers are expected back next year, probably to replace the roof, if more money comes in.[^3]

Today, the sources read here do not mention a rental or a public visiting program, and the register describes the lookout as not regularly staffed.[^1] The same 2011 visit found the trail in good condition, with new water bars.[^1] Whether visitors may go inside is not clear from these sources, so check with the Lolo National Forest before making the trip.[^1][^3]
