Mount Philo, in Chittenden County, Vermont, had a state fire lookout on its summit from the 1920s.[^1] In 1924 Mrs. Frances Humphreys deeded 150 acres of the mountain to the state as a place for recreation.[^3] State forest officials expected to post a watchman on the property and to put the tower to work spotting fires.[^1] A Vermont biennial report covering 1924 to 1926 says a new 60-foot tower, glass-enclosed at the top, went up on the mountain.[^1]

The wooden tower came down in 1938.[^1] A Burlington Free Press item from that year gave the reason as the Forest Service having granted the Bureau of Air Commerce permission to install an air beacon on the mountain.[^1] A new 80-foot steel tower had arrived, and a Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) camp from Underhill was due to erect it.[^1] A state report for the period ending June 30, 1940, says the CCC built a steel tower with a glass-enclosed cab, and that the Civil Aeronautics Authority held a lease for an airplane beacon on its top.[^1] The state's biennial report for 1953 and 1954 records a telephone installed in the tower and a line built from the tower to the camp.[^1]

By February 1973 the tower had fallen.[^1] A Burlington Free Press photo caption dated February 15, 1973, says hikers found it toppled off its concrete base.[^1] According to the caption, the fasteners on the supports seem to have been taken out on purpose so the tower would fall.[^1] The Forest Fire Lookout Association lists the tower as gone.[^2]

The Mt. Philo Inn's history describes a different tower.[^4] It says Frank Lewis built a 50-foot wooden tower on the summit with a 360-degree view, and that steel towers later replaced it.[^4] The history says none remain today, and it does not call the wooden tower a fire lookout.[^4] The sources do not say whether it was the same structure as the state's tower.[^4][^1]

Today the summit lies inside Mount Philo State Park.[^3] Visitors may drive up the summit road or leave their car at the bottom and climb on foot.[^3] The park's history says a Civilian Conservation Corps crew built the current access road and summit lodge in the early 1930s.[^3] No source describes a fire tower on the summit today, or whether any footings from the lookout survive.[^3]
