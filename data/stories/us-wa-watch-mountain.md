Watch Mountain stands about three miles north of Randle, in Lewis County, Washington, above the north side of the Cowlitz Valley.[^2][^1][^5] The National Historic Lookout Register notes that several lookouts once stood along that stretch of the valley, and that this is the only one still standing.[^1]

The state built the first lookout here in 1933, an L-4 ground cab placed at the east end of the ridge to protect private land.[^1][^3][^4] A cabin was added in 1940.[^1][^3] That first site was gone by the 1960s.[^3]

The current tower went up in 1963 on the western end of the ridge, replacing the earlier cab.[^1][^5] Its cab is a 14-by-14-foot Department of Natural Resources flattop with a catwalk.[^1][^4] The sources disagree on the rest of the physical details. Some give the tower's height as 20 feet, while TrailChick's header lists 42.[^2][^4][^5] They also differ on the gap between the two sites: the Former Fire Lookout Sites register says about a mile, while TrailChick and another history site put it at nearly two miles.[^3][^4][^5]

Staffing records are scattered. TrailChick reports that Charles Boyer and Irma Huntington staffed the first lookout in 1938.[^4] A 1955 newspaper item, reprinted on a history site, says the Forest Service had the lookout manned as a ground observer post, with aircraft sightings reported by radio to Randle headquarters for relay to Seattle.[^5] In the summer of 1967, Kenna Lagerquist of Randle spent part of her college break as a Department of Natural Resources lookout here.[^5] A 2003 visit report on the register found the tower unstaffed, and its visitor thought staffing was unlikely to return.[^1]

Today the tower works mainly as an electronics site.[^1] The 2003 visitor found the cab stripped of lookout gear except for a small shelf, which then held repeater cabinets, deep-cycle batteries and charge controllers.[^1] Solar panels and antennas cover the outside.[^1] The register records that the land has since been acquired by the Forest Service, and it suggests the electronics use probably saved the tower from removal.[^1]

Access is the hard part. The 2003 visitor found no signs pointing to the tower and walked about six miles past three locked gates to reach it.[^1] TrailChick says the approach is tricky and roundabout.[^4] Nothing in the sources describes public visiting. Since the tower is still used as a radio site, check with the Forest Service before setting out, and do not climb the cab or catwalk.
