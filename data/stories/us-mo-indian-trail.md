The Indian Trail Lookout stands near the center of the Indian Trail Conservation Area, in northeast Dent County between Salem and Steelville.[^3][^4] Private owners acquired the land in 1906 for rangeland, and grazing and burning went on there until the state bought the tracts between 1924 and 1927, largely to control wildfires.[^3]

In November 1926 a steel fire tower was ordered for the park, to be erected at the old Fishwater Iron Mine.[^2] A September 1927 clipping reported that the Game and Fish Department had recently finished one, with telephone links so that an alarm could spread quickly.[^2] That clipping called it the department's second tower of this kind, after the one at Deer Run State Park.[^2] The National Historic Lookout Register dates the tower to 1927 and calls it the second lookout in Missouri, a broader claim than the clipping makes.[^1][^2]

The sources disagree most about height. An August 1927 clipping gave 90 feet for a tower then under construction.[^2] The September 1927 clipping gave 73 feet to the deck, with a nine-foot steel enclosure at the top.[^2] An April 1928 clipping gave seventy-two feet, and a December 1935 clipping called the tower eighty feet.[^2] The state's 1939 to 1941 annual report and the register both give 85 feet.[^2][^1]

The April 1928 clipping names Keith McCanse, the state game and fish commissioner, who connected the park's most remote portions by phone and employed a forester.[^2] A December 1935 clipping describes a towerman kept on watch for the whole fire season.[^2]

The state's later annual reports record a water system at the tower site in fiscal 1958 to 1959. In fiscal 1973 to 1974 they record a three-bedroom residence at the tower site, plus a new parking lot and entrance road to the tower.[^2] Civilian Conservation Corps workers stationed there between 1933 and 1946 constructed most of the 55 miles of access roads.[^3]

The sources read do not say whether the tower was ever abandoned, burned or moved. The register says the tower is still in use for spotting fires, but that entry is undated, so it reflects the register's account rather than a confirmed present-day fact.[^1]

The Missouri Department of Conservation administers the 13,503-acre area.[^1][^3] Under its rules, visitors may hike and watch nature in the areas the department has opened to the public.[^3] A 2019 magazine article says that visitors at the top of the tower, near the center of the area, can look as far north as Rolla and as far south as Salem. A plaque at the base of the tower describes one stretch of the Trail of Tears that runs through the area.[^4] None of the sources read confirms whether the tower can be climbed today, so check with the department before going up.
