Tatoosh Ridge started as a camp in 1930, and a 1933 L-4 cab went up on the summit inside the Gifford Pinchot National Forest, just south of Mount Rainier National Park.[^1][^2][^4][^6] The nwhiker.com guide calls the site the former site of a fire lookout built in 1932, so the build year is not settled.[^5]

In 1931 the observer, Clarence Thompson, had to go home after he hurt his knee.[^3][^4] Panoramic photos were taken from the lookout in 1934.[^4]

In 1943 the Packwood district assigned its first woman lookout to Tatoosh Ridge, Seattle schoolteacher Martha Hardy.[^6] She wrote a book about that summer, which Willhite says was published by Macmillan in 1946.[^4][^6] Willhite also notes that a photo contributor's parents worked as lookouts in the 1940s.[^4]

In 1954 a newspaper item said Gilbert Lee, from Hawaii, was set to staff the Tatoosh lookout.[^3]

The Former Fire Lookout Sites register says the cab was removed in 1966, while Rex's page and Willhite say it was destroyed that year.[^1][^2][^4]

A visitor's account says the lookout is long gone, and that the author looked for traces of it at the summit and found none.[^6] Willhite notes a USGS benchmark placed in 1946 three feet south of the lookout, but no source I read says whether it is still there.[^4]

Willhite gives the route as 4.5 miles with 3,600 feet of gain, and says the access road is gravel that can get rough at the end.[^4] The visitor's account says the road was washed out before the trailhead on that trip.[^6] The summit lies inside the Tatoosh Wilderness, so this is a hike, not a drive-up.[^4] Plan for a long day on foot, and check road conditions before you go.
