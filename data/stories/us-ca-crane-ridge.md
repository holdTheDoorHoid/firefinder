Livermore Valley stockmen put a lookout on Crane Ridge, about 12 miles southeast of town. The ridge overlooks the range land of five counties.[^1] A 1921 article quoted on the Forest Lookouts page says sheepmen and cattlemen had organized the first association to prevent fires on stock ranges in the United States in 1904, and that the group set up this lookout.[^1]

The register gives 1914 as the build year and says volunteers built it, with labor from the Cattlemen's Protective Association of Livermore.[^2] The East Bay Times says the Stockmens Protective Association paid for it around 1916, working with Alameda County.[^3] The two sources disagree on the year.

According to the register, the building was a one-of-a-kind, two-story design of local make.[^2] The East Bay Times describes a cabin with no electricity or running water. Cables fastened to stakes driven deep into the ground anchored it, a precaution against the high winds that swept those heights.[^3] The paper also says a second-story room with windows facing all four directions was added later, and gives no date.[^3] Newspapers from 1941 describe a new tower room under way to improve the view, after wind damage to the building that winter.[^1]

According to the register, Henrietta Mendenhall was the best-known lookout from the 1920s until 1942. She was also the only known woman lookout in California at the time.[^2] The East Bay Times says the Mendenhall family filled the lookout job from 1926 to 1940,[^3] but a May 1942 clipping reports that she had returned to duty at the lookout.[^1]

In July 1927 a fire came within half a mile of the station. Mrs. Mendenhall kept to her post and relayed calls for help and reports on the fire.[^1] In July 1933 a grass fire threatened the lookout, and a newspaper credited the county fire patrol with saving the station from the flames.[^1]

The register says the lookout operated until 1973, when Gus Theil was the last person to work there. It also says the structure was removed at an unknown date before 1986.[^2] The East Bay Times quotes a member of the ranch family who remembered the post closing in 1969.[^3] The same article says vandals knocked the building over and set fire to it in the 1970s.[^3] The sources disagree about how and when the building came down.

Only the foundation remains, according to the register and the 2005 article, which also mentions some cable ties.[^2][^3] The register lists the site as on a private ranch.[^2] The sources do not document current access.
