A structure stood on the summit of Mount Zion in the Olympics by 1935.[^4] WillhiteWeb says it was built on or before that year, and June 20, 1935 panoramas show a stovepipe and a four-foot tripod, the tripod set 15 feet above ground.[^4]

According to the register, a frame cabin went up in 1943.[^1]

During the Second World War, Mount Zion was an Aircraft Warning Service post, known by the call sign "Charlie 4-9".[^4] A listing on the Washington Forest Lookouts site gives the activation date as August 16, 1942, under the Seattle Filter Center.[^3] The post used buildings owned by the Olympic National Forest, and the service added an 8-by-16-foot cabin.[^3][^4] A trail-guide caption calls it a year-round Aircraft Warning Service station, and a photo dated February 1943 is labelled as having been used as one.[^4] After the service closed, the Forest Service retained the site.[^3]

The register says the frame cabin was removed in the 1970s.[^1] Firelookout.com and the trail guide say it was destroyed in that decade.[^2][^4]

Today, the trail guide describes the top as a former lookout site.[^4] The guide gives Mount Zion Trail #836 as 1.8 miles long and counts it among the shortest paths to an Olympic summit.[^4] We found no source that describes what remains on the summit today.

Do not confuse this peak with a Mt. Zion in the Columbia River Gorge. Maps now call that one Biddle Butte.[^5]
