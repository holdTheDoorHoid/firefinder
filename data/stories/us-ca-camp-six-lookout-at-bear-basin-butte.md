Bear Basin Butte, on a narrow ridge above the Siskiyou Wilderness in Del Norte County, has carried more than one lookout. A tower went up on the butte itself in 1924, but lightning struck it and it burned in 1936.[^1] The building that stands there today came from somewhere else.

That structure started life a few ridges away, built as the Camp Six Lookout. The National Historic Lookout Register dates its construction to 1934, by a Civilian Conservation Corps crew, on a peak it calls Upper Coon Mountain.[^1] The Former Fire Lookout Sites register, cataloguing the same spot under the name Camp Six Mountain, instead records a lookout first built there in 1923, later replaced by a California Region 5 "BC-301" plan cabin on a 20-foot open timber tower; it does not give a year for that replacement.[^2] The two registers may simply be describing different stages of the same site's life, but the discrepancy is unresolved here.

By the 1950s the surrounding site had filled up with electronic equipment, and the lookout was decommissioned.[^1] It sat unused for decades. A restoration effort started in 1991, but vandals damaged the building again before the work was finished, and the Forest Service decided to pull it down rather than keep patching it in place.[^1] Instead, the agency dismantled the Camp Six cabin and moved it to Bear Basin Butte, reassembling it there. The Former Fire Lookout Sites register says the move happened in 1997, when the cabin was added to the Forest Service's recreation rental program and only the old tower piers were left behind at Camp Six.[^2] The recreation.gov listing for the rebuilt lookout instead gives 1996 for the move.[^3] Both sources agree on what happened; they differ by a year on when.

Today the relocated building is rented out as a pair with the nearby Pierson Cabin, a modern structure built in the 1990s in a matching 1930s style, with both administered by the Smith River National Recreation Area.[^1][^3] They are available for booking between July 1 and September 30 each year, sleeping up to eight people as standard (twelve for an extra fee): the cabin holds three double beds, one of them a bunk, and the lookout itself has a futon. There is no running water or electricity. The access road is rough, with its last half mile on steep gravel, and the Forest Service recommends a high-clearance four-wheel-drive vehicle, with chains and a shovel carried as a matter of course.[^3] The lookout was added to the National Historic Lookout Register on January 6, 1999.[^1]
