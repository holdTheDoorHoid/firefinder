Aeneas Mountain sits in Okanogan County, Washington. TrailChick's directions to it start from Loomis.[^2][^1]

The Forest Service was using a lookout here by 1914.[^3] A 1914 item quoted on the Washington lookout guide mentions the "Aeneas mountain lookout" of the Forest Service.[^3] TrailChick dates the site's start to a fire camp in 1914, but counts the lookout's unbroken summers from 1916.[^1] A one-room log cabin, built by the Forest Service in 1920, stood until 1956.[^2][^1]

In 1934 Civilian Conservation Corps crews built an L-4 cab on a 20-foot pole tower.[^1][^3] The Forest Service sold the tower to the Department of Natural Resources in 1954.[^1][^3] A lookout was completed on the mountain during the 1956 to 1958 biennium, and TrailChick says the cab was raised 20 feet in 1958.[^3][^1]

The structure that stood until 2024 went up in 1980.[^2][^1] The Department of Natural Resources built a timber tower with a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab.[^2][^3][^1] The register gives the tower as 40 feet tall, while TrailChick and the Washington lookout guide give 42 feet.[^2][^1][^3]

Staffing records disagree for 1955. TrailChick says Ray Kresek staffed the tower that year.[^1] The Washington lookout guide, quoting the Gazette-Tribune, names Willis Erwin for 1955 and 1956.[^3] Kresek, the Washington registrar, is also named as the nominator of the site's National Historic Lookout Register listing, which was registered on May 1, 1995.[^2] The register says the lookout was last staffed in 2020, and TrailChick calls it the last DNR lookout staffed in the state.[^2][^1] In August 2015 the lookout was evacuated over the risk of wildfire.[^3]

TrailChick reports that the lookout came down in November 2024 and is gone.[^1] The Forest Fire Lookout Association's Washington report says the state chose to replace it with a tower with cameras, and used a bulldozer to topple the lookout.[^4] The association's Washington list now marks the tower as gone.[^5] No source we read describes what, if anything, stands on the summit now.

Access is the hard part. TrailChick says the main road is DNR owned, but access has been controversial, and many have reported issues with road access and private landowners.[^1] The guide describes a second route, entirely on public land, that approaches from Sinlahekin Road. It gives 12.5 miles round trip with about 3,400 feet of climbing.[^1] With the lookout gone, there is no tower on the summit to climb.[^1]
