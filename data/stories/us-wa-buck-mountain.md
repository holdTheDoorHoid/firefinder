Buck Mountain sits in Okanogan County, Washington, about 13 miles west of Omak.[^1][^2] The sources differ on when the summit first held a lookout. The Register and firelookout.com date a crow's nest and log cabin there to 1919, a site the state developed.[^1][^2] Will Hite's page says the first lookout started around 1911, and TrailChick gives 1911 as the site's establishment year.[^3][^4]

In 1934 the crow's nest and cabin were removed and a 20-foot pole tower with an L-4 cab went up in their place.[^4] The present structure dates from 1961.[^1][^2][^4] It is a 14-by-14-foot flat-gable live-in cab for the Department of Natural Resources, set on a 20-foot timber tower.[^1][^4] Will Hite says the Department rebuilt the lookout in 1960 onto a 40-foot tower, which does not match the 20 feet the Register gives.[^1][^3] The Forest Lookouts page quotes a Department report saying its own carpentry crew finished nine lookouts in two years, one of them at Buck Mountain.[^5]

The first named keeper was Fred Cole, assigned for the 1920 fire season.[^2][^3][^5] Firelookout.com lists other staff from 1947 onward, ending with Sandra Mosby in 1960 and 1961.[^2] The Forest Lookouts page gives March 1942 to July 1943 as the active dates, and Will Hite says the summit was used by the Aircraft Warning Service in 1942.[^5][^3] Will Hite notes that the tower has not been staffed for decades.[^3]

The 2015 Okanogan Complex fire burned over the summit.[^3][^4] TrailChick, which calls it the Beaver Lake fire, says the flames scorched the legs but crews saved the structure.[^4] Will Hite describes a blast of heat that ignited the connecting wood at the bolts on one leg, and says that leg was burned nearly through.[^3] The Eastern Washington chapter's 2015 report says several tower members burned.[^6] Will Hite credits a 2016 FEMA Public Assistance grant with the repairs, after a contractor stabilized the tower for the winter.[^3]

The Register's June 2018 visit report records the tower as unstaffed, with new boards that were still unpainted and paint that was chipping.[^1] The Department keeps the cab for emergency use.[^1] TrailChick says it is sometimes still used after major lightning storms.[^4] The road up is on state land and is rough.[^3] Will Hite recommends high clearance and four-wheel drive.[^3] TrailChick says the catwalk is usually locked and gives the round trip as 3.9 miles.[^4] We found no rental listing, and the sources do not say whether the public may enter the cab. Check with the Department before any visit, and respect the locked catwalk.
