Gilman Peak, now inside Chino Hills State Park, carried a steel fire lookout that the register says two county fire departments funded together.[^1][^2] The state's own history dates the tower to 1935.[^1] It says the tower was finished in about six weeks and that its dedication was set for Saturday, September 21, 1935.[^1] A Los Angeles Times item from September 22, 1935 reports that the dedication ceremonies had opened, which fits that date.[^3] The register gives 1937 instead.[^2]

The cab was about 14 feet square and, by one 1938 account, sat about 30 feet in the air.[^1] The tower itself was made of steel.[^1]

The first person to man the tower was Buell Castile, who seems to have stayed only briefly.[^1] In July 1936 it was announced that Harvey George French had been appointed lookout.[^1] The register calls French the first operator when the tower was completed in 1937.[^2] The two accounts disagree.

Glenn Downer and his wife lived in the cab, and a 1938 Los Angeles Times article featured them.[^1] Lois Smith, who had spent six years in other lookouts, came in the early 1950s.[^1] In June 1953 David and Myrtle McKenzie were found unconscious inside, apparently from carbon monoxide given off by a gas appliance. David died. Myrtle lived.[^1]

The state closed this station and the Bolero Peak station in 1967 because the budget had no money to run them.[^1] That October the Paseo Grande fire broke out in Corona, just east of the closed lookout, and crossed into Orange County.[^1] At least one state assemblyman was quoted as saying both stations could have helped with containment.[^1]

In July 1982 a Chino Champion report and the State Parks history both describe the abandoned tower as still standing. Its bottom ladder section had been cut off to discourage climbers.[^1][^3]

The register says the tower was removed after 1966 and gives no exact year.[^2] The State Parks author says many abandoned lookouts were vandalized and eventually destroyed. The author is not sure when the last remains disappeared and guesses it might have been shortly after 1982.[^1]

The register places the site in Chino Hills State Park.[^2] The State Parks history says there is not much left at the top, apart from occasional hikers and bikers.[^1] No source we read describes footings or other remains on the summit, and none gives a current access rule for the peak. With no tower left, there is nothing to climb.[^1][^2]
