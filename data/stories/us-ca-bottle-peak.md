The Bottle Peak lookout was built in the spring of 1912 by District Ranger Ed D. Bish, who paid for it, designed it and put it up.[^1] A 1912 Los Angeles Times report, as quoted on a lookout history site, calls him Bush.[^5]

The cab was a 7-by-7-foot wood structure with a hip roof, set on the ground rather than on legs.[^1] Climbers reached it by a 29-foot ladder up the granite summit.[^1] It was one of two lookouts that launched the Cleveland National Forest's detection network, and a similar structure went up on Hot Springs Mountain at the same time.[^1][^2] Residents gave the peak its name, comparing the new station to a bottle perched on a rock.[^3]

The 1912 report says the lookout had two telephones.[^5] A 1938 National Geodetic Survey data sheet marked it abandoned, yet sources place it still standing in 1950.[^1][^2] Peakbagging gives 1937 as the abandonment year.[^4] Historian Robert Lerner writes that a fire destroyed it during the 1950s.[^3]

The concrete foundation is the main remnant, and the register and FFLA-SDRC both say it is still there.[^1][^2] Peakbagging's photo caption says nothing remains, but the same page notes that concrete steps survive.[^4] The site lies in a 418-acre open space preserve that Lerner says the County of San Diego has owned since 2013.[^3] The register lists the City of Escondido as administrator, so the sources differ on who manages the land.[^1]

Elevation figures vary too. The register gives 1,725 feet, while FFLA-SDRC and Peakbagging give 2,136.[^1][^2][^4] A Valley Center trails group took 14 people to the summit on one outing, and Lerner calls the climb strenuous.[^3] The article mentions a county management plan for the preserve but gives no visitor rules, so ask the county before going.[^3]
