Cameron Lookout stands on the summit of Mount Burke, in the Livingstone Range of Kananaskis Country in Alberta.[^2][^4] Alberta Historic Places dates the first lookout to 1929, when it was called Burke Lookout.[^2] A Canada Lookouts page that cites a 1998 book also gives 1929 for the first structure.[^5]

A 1937 Calgary Herald article says the cabin was built in 1930, when aerial patrols were judged too expensive and Mount Burke was picked for its height and access.[^3][^5] The register says the Canadian Forest Service surveyed and built it in the late 1920s or early 1930s, with provincial staff.[^1] A 2025 blog credits the Alberta Forest Service instead.[^4]

Cut into 3.7 m (12 ft) lengths, the lumber went up Mount Burke on pack ponies.[^5] Wooden shutters cover windows about 0.9 m (3 ft) high on the west, south and east walls.[^5] Guy lines to the treeline helped steady the cabin in bad windstorms.[^5]

The sources give its height differently. The register says 2,504 m (8,214 ft).[^1] Alberta Historic Places says 2,540 m (8,330 ft) and calls it the highest lookout in Canada.[^2] A 1930 Herald item gives 2,542 m (8,340 ft).[^5]

A ranger from Pekisko Cabin packed water, fuel and supplies up once a week, and a telephone line linked the lookout to Pekisko Cabin.[^5] Alberta Historic Places says the observer had to gather his own stove wood, and at Cameron that meant hiking down to a point below the treeline.[^2]

The lookout closed in 1953, and two lower sites, Raspberry Ridge and Hailstone Butte, replaced it.[^2][^4] A 2020 blog post gives 1954 from another blog, but its author found no firsthand source for that date.[^3]

A 2025 visit found it standing empty, with a steel cable running down to the treeline.[^4] The same post includes a 2006 photo and says the lookout has deteriorated much since then.[^4] When it was registered in 1996, the register said it remained in reasonably good condition, and that the Alberta chapter of the Forest Fire Lookout Association would survey and maintain it.[^1] Alberta Historic Places says it can still be seen with binoculars from Highway 2.[^2]

The old approach began at the lot outside Cataract Creek campground. The 2013 floods appear to have wiped out much of it, and the route now follows a cutline from the parking area.[^3] The author recorded 12.5 km round trip, about four hours up and three down, on a trail that was well marked the whole way.[^3]
