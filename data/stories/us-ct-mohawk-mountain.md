The Mohawk Mountain lookout sits in Litchfield County, Connecticut.[^1] Its first summit tower went up in 1922, after a fire destroyed 5,000 acres of woodland.[^3] A 1922 report from the state park and forest commission says the tower came about through the help of the White Memorial Foundation, which had given the Mohawk State Forest to the state.[^2]

The first tower was built of wood, and a 1924 newspaper item notes that a watchman was kept there during the fire season.[^2] In 1937 a Hartford Courant item reported that a new steel tower had replaced the wooden one, that the Forest Fire Service kept an observer there in every month except winter, and that a road built by the Civilian Conservation Corps led to the summit.[^2]

The register says aircraft detection phased the tower out in 1988, and that it was Connecticut's final fire tower in operation.[^1] A Cold War site history gives a different timeline, saying the fire tower reportedly ran seasonally up until the seventies.[^5] That same history suggests the microwave tower replaced the original tower, which leaves the end date uncertain.[^5]

Today the summit carries a relay tower built for radio signals.[^4] The state's forest page says AT&T erected the present tower for that purpose.[^4] The register describes a metal cab measuring 7 by 7 feet, which rests on a far larger microwave tower.[^1]

The register also says the original tower, without its cab, still stands nearby and welcomes the public as an observation site.[^1] A January 2017 visit report says the original tower and a former observation tower on Booth Hill have both been removed.[^1] The register itself disagrees with that account, so the original tower's status is unresolved.[^1]

A visitor in January 2017 found the tower in surprisingly good shape, with its windows all intact.[^1] The same visit notes that the cab sits atop a large cell tower, and that Connecticut has stopped staffing its fire towers.[^1] Getting to the summit is easy to follow, since the road and trails are clearly marked.[^1] None of the sources I read says the cab is open to visitors today, so treat it as a site to view from the ground, not to climb.
