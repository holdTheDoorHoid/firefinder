Burley Mountain Lookout stands in Lewis County, Washington, inside the Gifford Pinchot National Forest.[^1] One listing puts it about 10 miles south-southeast of Randle, while a 1960 newspaper says 28 miles south.[^3][^2]

The register calls it a ground house rather than a tower. It is 14 feet square, has no catwalk, follows the L-4 design, and dates from 1934.[^1][^4] The sources I read do not say why it was placed on this peak.[^1]

A fire watch was on the mountain before the cab was built.[^2] A 1930 newspaper notice said K.I. Peters would occupy Burley Mountain for the summer, and a June 1931 notice put him there again.[^2] A May 1960 Daily Chronicle notice called Burley the district's first lookout, set to open that June. The sources do not reconcile that with the earlier notices.[^2]

In May 1964 the forest announced that its 37 regular and emergency lookouts would be cut to six regularly manned ones, and Burley Mountain was one of the six kept.[^2] In July 1968 a teacher from Concrete, Washington, staffed the lookout for the summer.[^2] The Daily Chronicle gave her name as both Darleen and Charleen Kemper.[^2] In 1972 Jan Grose, 23, of Mossyrock, staffed the one lookout still operating in the Randle Ranger District.[^2]

The National Forest Foundation says Burley was staffed until 1974.[^5] An undated firelookout.com listing says the cab has sat unused for years and is being restored.[^3] The Forest Service page, last updated August 24, 2026, says it is one of three lookouts left on the forest and still used to spot fires.[^4] The sources differ on how actively it is used today.[^3][^4]

An undated National Forest Foundation article says Filson and the foundation are working with the Gifford Pinchot National Forest to restore the lookout over the next two years, to make it available for public rental.[^5] I did not find a rental open yet. The register gives the elevation as 5,154 feet, while the foundation and the 1968 newspaper give about 5,310 feet.[^1][^5][^2]

Today the Forest Service lists it as open for day visits only, between sunrise and sunset.[^4] Hikers take the half-mile trail No. 256A to it, starting from a lot in the saddle just under the summit.[^4] The Forest Service advises leaving the car below the lookout by the gate and covering the final 300 feet on foot.[^4] The Forest Service page does not say whether visitors may climb the lookout. Because the cab is still used for fire detection, please do not try to climb it.[^4]
