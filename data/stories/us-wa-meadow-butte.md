Meadow Butte's lookout is a small crow's-nest cab on top of a pine tree, about six miles west of Glenwood in Klickitat County, Washington.[^1][^4] The register and the trail guide give the tree as 86 feet tall, while the Forest Lookouts page puts the cab 90 feet above the ground.[^1][^2][^3]

The register says the cab was built in the early 1940s to watch over railroad logging, with a tent cabin for living quarters.[^1] Panoramic photos taken from the top in July 1941 show the cab was standing by then.[^3][^5] WillhiteWeb's history says the first user was J. Neal Lumber Company, which needed to watch its railroad logging sites for fire.[^5]

The Washington Division of Forestry took ownership in 1944 and fitted the lookout with a fire finder and radio.[^1][^5] A 1944 state report also mentions a new lookout house at Meadow Butte.[^3] WillhiteWeb guesses it was the cabin at the base, but the sources do not confirm that.[^5]

The register lists the site as Washington state land, while the trail guide calls the land private, so check who manages it before you plan a trip.[^1][^2]

The trail guide says many women staffed the lookout in the 1940s.[^2] Newspaper items quoted by the Forest Lookouts page show Dolores Coate stationed there in 1948, Juanita Ward due on the lookout in 1955, and Dorothy Lewis and Kathleen Ladiges covering in 1956 while Sylvia Anrig was away.[^3] A November 1948 Goldendale Sentinel article, quoted by the trail guide, recalled Nancy Pearson going up and down an 80-foot ladder five times a day, every day of the week, four years earlier.[^2][^3] The guide's list spells her name Pierson.[^2]

A DNR employee remembered that hauling equipment up the tree each year, with ropes, was a real chore. The ladder stood three feet out from the trunk.[^5]

The lookout was abandoned in 1958, and the register and the fire lookout page say the structure still remains.[^1][^4] The ground cabin was relocated to the DNR facility in Glenwood, where it served as a storm shelter, a DNR employee recalled.[^5] A 2011 DNR regional report said efforts were under way to decide its future, and that it needed to move to a public place.[^6]

The elevation is given as 3,607 feet by the register, 3,621 feet by the trail guide, and 3,620 feet by the fire lookout page.[^1][^2][^4]

Visiting means hiking. The trail guide describes about three miles up an old logging road from a berm, 6.8 miles round trip, and says the route from the berm is a bit overgrown.[^2] Please do not climb the ladder. The trail guide's photos are from 2018, so the cab's current condition is not confirmed.[^2]
