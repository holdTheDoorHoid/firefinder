Bald Mountain is a large granite dome east of Shaver Lake, in the Sierra National Forest of Fresno County.[^1][^3] The summit sits at about 7,800 feet.[^1] The National Historic Lookout Register says the first structure there was a cabin dating to 1905, and that a wooden tower replaced it in 1911.[^1] The register describes the current tower as a 20-foot H-brace metal structure, built in 1934.[^1]

A California lookout history tells the story differently. It describes an 18-foot, 6-inch H-brace steel tower with a 14-by-14 cab, built by Civilian Conservation Corps crews in 1936.[^2] The two accounts disagree on the year and on the height, and neither explains the gap.[^1][^2]

The summit was staffed by July 1912, when its lookout sighted a fire on the south slope of Kaiser Crest.[^2] Investigators traced it to a campfire.[^2] Three men from Fowler admitted guilt under the state fire law. Each was first fined $25, and after they pleaded poverty the total fell to $30.[^2] A 1913 newspaper note places a lookout on "Baldy," near Shaver.[^2]

The most dramatic night came in September 1921.[^2] Lightning struck the tower and the cabin.[^2] Lookout T.E. Parker was in the cabin rather than the tower, and the strike knocked him senseless.[^2] The tower was wrecked, and the telephone was destroyed.[^2] Parker had worked the post for about ten seasons.[^2] The nervous shock led to his release in 1922, and he died in January 1924.[^2] O.W. Barnhart was the lookout in 1927.[^2]

The last season with a lookout on duty was 1979, and the post was given up that October.[^3] The register recorded the tower on August 30, 2009, as US 823, CA 88, after a nomination by Dave Bula.[^1] The Forest Fire Lookout Association's California list still shows it as standing.[^4] Photos dated August 2008 show the windows broken out, and the photo page says a visitor can climb into the cab.[^3] Nothing newer that we found describes the cab.[^3] We do not recommend climbing it.

According to that 2008 page, an off-highway vehicle route to the summit is busy on weekends.[^3] Visitors without a suitable vehicle can walk the road marked 9S43 instead.[^3] The U.S. Forest Service administers the site, and the register lists the High Sierra Ranger District as a cooperator.[^1] We found no current access notice, so check with the district before heading up.
