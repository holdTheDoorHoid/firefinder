Coolwater Lookout is one of the oldest fire-watch sites in the Lochsa-Selway country, with origins going back to 1915, when a 12-by-12-foot frame cabin went up on the summit.[^1][^2][^3] Sources differ on the exact year the first cabin went up—1915 or 1916.[^1][^2][^3] Idaho Fire Lookouts records G.A. Burrows as the earliest known observer, staffing it starting that year.[^2] In its earliest years, one of the lookout's jobs was relaying heliograph messages from other lookouts that didn't have telephone lines.[^3]

The frame cabin was replaced by a larger 14-by-14-foot log cabin, which different sources date to either 1926 or 1931.[^1][^2] An L-4 cab then replaced the log cabin as the active lookout structure—Idaho Fire Lookouts and firelookout.com both date this to 1931, while the National Historic Lookout Register instead gives 1936; firelookout.com's own account skips the log cabin and describes the L-4 as replacing the frame cabin directly.[^1][^2][^3] In 1953, that L-4 cab was moved about 100 feet down the mountain, and the Forest Service built the current structure in its place: a 10-foot treated-timber tower with a hip-roof L-4 cab and catwalk.[^1][^2][^3] The relocated older cab is still there today, in rough shape, and one former lookout recalled it having served as his kids' playhouse in the 1980s.[^2]

Known staff stretch across decades: Burrows in the mid-1910s, Ernie "Tarzan" Bohn in 1929, Bob and Kris Anderson through much of the late 1970s and 1980s, Bill Harlow from 1995 to 2002, and Tom Vandewater, who staffed the tower from 2010 into at least 2025.[^2]

Coolwater was added to the National Historic Lookout Register on April 6, 2009, nominated by Gary Weber.[^1] It remains an active, staffed Forest Service lookout on the Nez Perce National Forest, about 24 miles east of Kooskia, at roughly 6,929 feet elevation.[^1][^3] Visitors have reached it by a rough road requiring high-clearance or four-wheel drive, or by hiking up from a saddle a couple of miles out; because it's a working lookout and someone's seasonal home, visitors should expect it to be occupied and should be respectful if they stop by.[^2]
