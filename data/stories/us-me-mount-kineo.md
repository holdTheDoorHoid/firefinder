Mount Kineo rises above Moosehead Lake on a peninsula in Somerset County, Maine.[^3][^2] The lookout tower on its summit went up in 1917. It still stands, though the cab that once sat on top is gone.[^1][^2]

A lookout house had already been built near the summit in 1910, according to that year's Forest Commissioner report.[^2] The 1917 steel tower was erected together with the Maine Central Railroad and the Ricker Hotel Company, and the Kennebec Valley Protective Association paid for the labor of erection.[^2]

Sources disagree on its height. A June 1917 newspaper item gave 40 feet, a 1917 state report said about 65 feet, and the 1917 annual report and the 1920 inventory both say 64 feet.[^2] The Bangor Daily News gives 64 feet in 2021, citing records from the Maine Chapter of the Forest Fire Lookout Association.[^3] A 1923 Maine Forest Service map shows a 360-degree panorama centered on the fire tower.[^5]

In 1917 Chief Fire Warden Louis Oakes of Greenville was in charge of the station, and Melvin S. Blethan of Foxcroft was the watchman.[^2] In April 1918 the Daily Kennebec Journal reported that Alice Henderson, a 21-year-old from Gardiner, would be the first woman watcher in a New England lookout.[^2] The paper called it a federal station, while the Forest Lookouts history lists the Maine Forestry District as the agency, so the owner is unclear.[^2] We found no notable fire tied to this tower in the sources we read.

The Forest Fire Lookout Association marks towers that keep their structure but lose the cab with an asterisk, and Kineo carries that mark.[^1] A Maine Trail Finder story says the Maine Forest Service used the tower through the 1960s and that it has since been converted with a viewing platform.[^4] The Bangor Daily News calls it a former fire observation tower that now works as a viewing platform for visitors.[^3] We did not find when the cab came down or when the conversion happened.

Mount Kineo State Park covers 800 acres of the peninsula and was established in 1990.[^3] One line of the article says the mountain is accessible by water only, but another notes a narrow strip of land to the mainland. Because the same piece says there is no road access to the park, we describe it that way.[^3] Trails from the park entrance climb the mountain, and the Bridle Trail runs 0.4 mile to the summit tower.[^3] That tower is made of steel with railings and a wood deck.[^3]
