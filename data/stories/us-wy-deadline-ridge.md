Deadline Ridge Lookout stands at about 10,000 feet in the Wyoming Range, roughly 45 minutes southwest of Big Piney in Sublette County, on Bridger-Teton National Forest land.[^1][^2][^3] It is an 11-foot-square, single-story cabin of the Plan 80 type, and its south roof once carried a large 8 so that pilots could identify it from the air.[^3]

Sources do not agree on when it was built. The National Historic Lookout Register gives circa 1940.[^1] The county says the Civilian Conservation Corps built it in 1941.[^2] The restoration brochure cites Forest Service records for a 1941 build with $483 in Forest Service funds, but says CCC crews may have built it, which was never documented.[^3]

The cab was not always on this ridge.[^3] The brochure says this lookout was once called the Castle Fire lookout and was moved from a site called Red Castles on Bare Pass, sometime before 1955.[^3] The register does not mention the move.[^1]

The register says little is known about who staffed the lookout.[^1] The brochure describes a heavy wooden table fastened to the floor for use with the fire finder.[^3] No specific fire is recorded in the sources read.

Lookout use wound down in the 1960s.[^1][^2] The brochure gives its use as circa 1941 to 1964.[^3] The county says it was used into the late 1960s and then decommissioned as aircraft and newer communication tools sped up fire detection and reporting.[^2] The register says use continued into the 1960s and perhaps the early 1970s.[^1] The brochure says aircraft patrols had replaced every Bridger-Teton lookout by the early 1970s.[^3] Before the restoration, the structure had been used as a communications site and had fallen into disrepair.[^4][^2]

Volunteers from the Sublette County Historic Preservation Board began in August 2015 with two weekends of clearing out debris, removing the old windows, shutters and door, and repainting the outside.[^4] By September 2016 the door and eleven of the fifteen windows had been repaired and put back.[^5] The brochure dates the project 2016 to 2019, while the county says it lasted three years.[^3][^2] Forest Service archaeologists helped identify the historic colors, and the volunteers repainted the lookout to match, including the large white 8 on the roof.[^2] The brochure, however, describes the 8 as yellow.[^3]

The county held a public open house there in August 2019.[^2] Visitors are asked to sign the guest book inside and to close the door when they leave.[^3] The last stretch is a road of about half a mile to the ridgetop, and the county recommends high-clearance vehicles.[^3][^2] The site has no restroom or water service.[^3]
