The tower by the Sorting Gap Marina on the Rainy River in Fort Frances, Ontario, was first built at Pal Lake, near Atikokan.[^1][^2] The sources disagree about when it was built and what for. The marker in Fort Frances says it was one of the filler sites in a Royal Canadian Air Force radar programme begun in 1959 to cover areas the long-range Pinetree Line radars did not scan. The project was cancelled in January 1964, and although construction was 95 per cent complete, none of the filler sites became operational.[^1] The National Historic Lookout Register instead dates the tower to the 1930s and calls it a forest fire lookout and radar tower.[^2] A Times column reports that the Department of Lands and Forests used it as a fire lookout for at least a few years after buying it from the air force.[^3]

Both the marker and the town give the tower a height of 33 m (100 ft), though the marker's account of the Pal Lake radar tower gives about 21 m (70 ft).[^1][^4] The cupola is square, which one landscape architect on the project found unusual, and the Times describes it as red and white.[^3]

In 1964 the tower was sold to Ontario's Department of Lands and Forests, and a tower at the nearby Caland Ore site made it redundant.[^1][^3] In 1972 James Wrench Towse, Fort Frances' industrial development commissioner, paid a $1 moose-hide cheque, and the tower was moved to Point Park as a tourist attraction.[^5][^4] The minister of natural resources, Leo Bernier, opened it on 1 July 1972, and more than 1,000 people climbed it that summer.[^3]

The Fort Frances Museum was established in 1978, and the tower operated as an attraction until 2002. That year it closed over safety concerns about the building code, including stair slopes and railings.[^5] In 2009 the tower was moved from Point Park to the Sorting Gap Marina.[^4]

The tower now stands on the west side of the Sorting Gap Marina, along the La Verendrye Parkway.[^1] The town says that when the marina is open in summer, visitors can buy a pass for the day's access code and walk up the tower, although its page places that sentence under the Hallett entry.[^4] A Times column notes that access was limited to the summer months and was stopped one spring because of COVID-19.[^3] Check with the town before you go, as the arrangements may have changed. The marker assumes that all the gap-filler sites were eventually dismantled, and it does not say what, if anything, remains at Pal Lake today.[^1]
