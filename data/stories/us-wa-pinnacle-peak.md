Pinnacle Peak, also called Mount Peak or Mount Pete, rises two miles south-southeast of Enumclaw in King County, Washington.[^2][^5] The mountain was once called Mt. Pete, after Pete Blascheck, who lived on its south side and whom people thought owned it.[^4]

The first lookout was a pole platform tower, 30 feet tall.[^1][^2] The register and firelookout.com date it to 1928, but a visitor page says the Washington Forest Fire Association built it in May 1929.[^1][^2][^4] The same page says the association put up a taller 60-foot tower in 1934.[^4] A cabin followed in 1942.[^1][^2]

In 1950, a 20-foot wooden DNR tower with a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab was built.[^4][^3] The 1950 date and the 20-foot height come from the visitor page and a 2018 report.[^3][^4] firelookout.com dates a 20-foot tower to 1949,[^2] and the register describes a 40-foot tower instead.[^1]

Full-time staffing ended in 1964, and the lookout was abandoned that October.[^4] It was manned for four days in 1965.[^4][^2]

The tower came down on May 13, 1966.[^4] A 2018 report also says it was torn down in 1966.[^3] The DNR had wanted to keep the tower on standby, but vandalism and break-ins made removal necessary.[^4] The register gives 1971 as the removal year, which we could not reconcile with the others.[^1]

The site is now a King County park, and the county has acquired more land around the summit over time.[^4] A 2014 photo caption reads "Lunch on the summit in 2014. Bench stretches between lookout footings," but no source we read describes the footings today.[^4]

A 20-foot replica with a viewing platform and a windowless cab was built in 2021.[^2] A Washington Trails Association trip report from November 2021 says the new lookout now stands on Mount Peak and is open to visitors.[^5] firelookout.com says the replica serves as a point of interest and is not staffed to spot fires.[^2]

The site is a public park, and the trail is well used.[^4][^5] The south trailhead, off SE Mud Mountain Road, has a gravel lot and bathrooms.[^4][^5] From there the Washington Trails Association puts the hike at about 3.6 miles with roughly 1,200 feet of climbing.[^5] A visitor page gives a range of 1 to 2 miles, depending on the route.[^4]
