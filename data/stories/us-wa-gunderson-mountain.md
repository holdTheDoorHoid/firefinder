Gunderson Mountain is a low, lone peak about four miles north-northwest of Forks, in Clallam County on Washington's coast.[^1][^2][^4]

A road to the lookout site was cleared in 1947, before winter set in, so that the installation could be finished before the 1948 fire season.[^3][^4] In 1948 a 75-foot surplus Navy steel tower with a 14-by-18-foot ground house went up.[^1][^4] It brought radio communication to the area for the first time.[^3][^4]

The sources record one notable night in 1951.[^4] During the Great Forks Fire, lookout Glen S. Merchant was on the overnight shift and saw a red glow roughly 18 miles northwest of Forks.[^4] He reported it to the district warden.[^4] The fire had started at Camp Creek, out of the smoldering remains of an earlier summer fire behind Heckelsville.[^4]

Named lookouts appear for 1955 and 1956, and again from 1961 through 1965.[^4] Nancy Gorbin is listed for 1955 and 1956, Beth Velie for 1961, Lynn Hanchett for the start of 1962, Wayne Lewis for 1963, and Linda Fisk Pickering for the summers of 1964 and 1965.[^4] A 1964 newspaper item, as quoted on a Washington lookouts listing, said seven lookouts would be manned that June, with Gunderson among them.[^3]

Accounts of the structure differ.[^4] Beth Velie recalled a cab of about 10 by 10 feet and an 80-foot ladder.[^4] Linda Pickering remembered a 7-foot square viewing cab and two 40-foot sections of ladder.[^4]

The Former Fire Lookout Sites register says the tower and cabin were gone by 1969.[^1] WillhiteWeb says the lookout was dismantled by then, and a Washington lookouts listing marks the site as removed.[^4][^3]

The lookout is gone, and the summit has since been used for radio.[^1][^4][^5] WillhiteWeb describes an open clearing with communication structures, and says the author could not find any footings or eyebolts.[^4] In July 2020 the Forks Forum reported that the Clallam County Amateur Radio Club was preparing three new antennas for a repeater moving to the site.[^5] The site is leased by Forks Community Hospital.[^5]

The route runs up a DNR public access road and then a gated road.[^4] WillhiteWeb says the land beside the gated road is Rayonier timber land, and that legal recreation there requires a Rayonier recreation permit.[^4] We found no statement about whether the summit itself is open to the public today.[^4]
