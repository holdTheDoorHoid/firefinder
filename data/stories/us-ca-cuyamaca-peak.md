Cuyamaca Peak rises to 6,512 feet in San Diego County, and a fire lookout stood on its summit from 1918 until 1985.[^1][^2] The first tower was a 10-foot timber tower with an enclosed cab, put up that year.[^1][^2]

In the 1930s it was replaced by a 30-foot steel K-brace tower with a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab.[^1][^2] The register and the San Diego chapter give different design codes for that cab, BC-301 and C3.[^1][^2] The register's Built field gives only a rough circa-1930s date for the steel tower, so the exact year is unclear.[^1]

The peak became part of Cuyamaca Rancho State Park in 1933.[^1][^2] According to the register, the Cleveland National Forest ran the lookout through 1975, and the San Diego chapter says operation continued deep into the 1970s.[^1][^2]

Old newspaper items reprinted on a lookout history site give a few glimpses of the people who worked here.[^3] In August 1939 a second lieutenant in the Marine Corps Reserve was killed when his plane crashed and set a brush patch on fire, and a forest lookout on the peak located the wreck.[^3] In October 1939 a lookout named C.A. Brown was trapped in his station for hours, because his door opened outward and a 74-mile-an-hour gale held it shut.[^3] In March 1944 two lookouts, Roy Pogue and Art Towne, were snowbound on the peak until a ski patrol relief party reached them.[^3]

The register says the tower was torn down and burned in 1985.[^1] The San Diego chapter is less specific: it puts the loss either in a wildfire or in a demolition by the mid-1980s, so the cause is unresolved.[^2]

A Friends of the Descanso Library history of the lookouts says the Cuyamaca lookout no longer exists.[^4] Wikipedia describes a hike of about 3.5 miles, popular all year, that starts at Paso Picacho Campground and ends at the summit, which lies inside Cuyamaca Rancho State Park.[^5] None of the sources we read describes what, if anything, remains on the summit today.
