Silver Star Mountain is a bald, exposed peak in Skamania County in southwest Washington.[^1] It lies in the Gifford Pinchot National Forest.[^5] The 1902 Yacolt Burn reached parts of Skamania County. Wikipedia says 16 more fires in the decades that followed led government agencies to put a lookout on the peak.[^2]

The earliest lookout reference we found is a 1929 newspaper item, quoted by Forest Lookouts. In it, the lookout at Silver Star reported that a steep, timbered ridge could carry a fire quickly into the forest if the wind changed.[^5] The register and firelookout.com both describe an L-4 cab built in 1934.[^3][^4] Wikipedia dates a lookout on the higher north peak to 1952 and says it was removed by 1969.[^2] A 1952 newspaper item, however, says the lookout had already been kept for five years by then, so the dates do not agree.[^5]

In October 1952 the Longview Daily News reported, as quoted by Forest Lookouts, that Anne Donnelly, a teacher at R.A. Long High School, had kept the lookout for five years with her two sons.[^5] A 1954 item put her time there at seven summer seasons.[^5] In 1959 a Mrs. Robert B. Donnelly was said to have served as lookout for several years, and in 1962 Vernon Kinney worked the lookout for the summer.[^5]

From March 1942 to October 1943 the site served as an Aircraft Warning Service station.[^5] Money from the AWS also went to sleeping quarters and a woodshed.[^5] When that service ended, the Forest Service kept the site as a fire detection point.[^5]

A 1954 article describes a rustic new house, 15 feet 4 inches square, built in July and August to replace the old 1934 house. It gives the flat roof as 22 feet square.[^5] The register instead says an R-6 flat cab replaced the L-4 in 1955 and was later removed.[^3] The two dates differ by a year, and the sources do not say which is right.[^3][^5]

Firelookout.com marks the site as gone and leaves the end of the later cab open.[^4] Wikipedia says the north-peak lookout was gone by 1969.[^2] The register gives no removal date.[^3]

The summit is a hiking destination today.[^1] From the top, the Oregon Hikers guide says, five Cascade volcanoes come into view in clear weather.[^1] The same guide treats the summit as a former lookout site, pointing to the remains of a foundation on top.[^1] The north approach currently needs high clearance and all-wheel drive.[^1] A trail from the parking lot follows the abandoned lookout road for part of the way, and the Will Hite guide advises a high-clearance vehicle on the access road.[^6] No source describes a cab, tower or other structure standing there now.
