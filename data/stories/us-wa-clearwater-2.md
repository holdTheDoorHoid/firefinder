## Clearwater Lookout

Before there was a tower, there was a trapper's cabin. Around 1908 the Forest Service converted an old trapper's cabin into the Clearwater Ranger Station, in the Blue Mountains south of Pomeroy, Washington; within a year the surrounding glade had been fenced off as a ranger station pasture, with corrals for sheep built nearby about a year after that.[^1] In 1928 Ranger Grover Blake put up a three-room house there from scrap and donated lumber, and moved a one-room cabin down from Teal Spring to serve as guards' quarters.[^1]

The steel tower that still stands came five years later. A Civilian Conservation Corps camp of about 200 men worked the Asotin District for roughly four months in 1933, and among their projects was a new garage and steel tower at Clearwater.[^1] It was an Aermotor-brand MC-39 tower, one of a run of prefabricated steel towers the Aermotor Company of Chicago supplied to the Pacific Northwest starting in the 1920s and in greater numbers through the following decade, mostly east of the Cascades.[^2] Sources don't agree on its height: the Umatilla National Forest's own inventory calls it 90 feet, lookout historian Ray Kresek gives 94 feet, and firelookout.com gives 82.[^2][^3] The ground cabin at the tower's base, where overnight guests stay today, followed two years later, in 1935, also CCC-built.[^2][^3] Oddly, the lookout's own national register entry lists 1938 as the "Built" year even though its own description credits the 1933 CCC crew with the tower — likely a mix-up between the tower's construction and some other milestone; this story follows the CCC account.[^2]

By 1939 Clearwater had become the headquarters for the district's Protective Assistant, a role it took over from Diamond Peak, and the Forest Service ran new telephone line to it with a double switchboard.[^1]

The tower last did real work in August 2021, when its height made it useful as a temporary radio repeater during the Lick Creek (Dry Gulch) fire in southeast Washington.[^2] It isn't staffed during fire season anymore, but the Forest Service keeps it available for extreme fire conditions, and the tower itself is closed to public access.[^2][^4] OpenStreetMap also tags the structure as access restricted.[^5] The cabin, though, is rented out year-round through Recreation.gov: a kitchen-and-bedroom cabin furnished with four extra-long single beds, propane heat, lights and a cook stove, no running water on site, reachable by car from June through October and by snowshoe, ski or snowmobile the rest of the year. It was refreshed inside and out in 2023.[^4]
