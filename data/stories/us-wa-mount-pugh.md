Mount Pugh rises in the Mt. Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest, in Snohomish County, Washington, about 13 miles southeast of Darrington.[^1][^2] A 1950 survey description puts it about 16 miles southeast of Darrington in a straight line.[^3]

In 1916 the summit was first set up with a tent camp, a rock platform and an alidade, a sighting tool.[^1][^4] A D-6 cupola cabin followed in 1919.[^1][^2][^4] Willhite says the peak was known early on for lightning strikes, and that lightning destroyed the cupola in 1922, when a new structure went up the same year.[^4] The register and Firelookout.com instead say lightning destroyed the cabin and that it was rebuilt in 1927.[^1][^2]

Supplies went up a hand-powered tramway in two legs, one 1,950 feet long and the other 2,700 feet.[^4]

Willhite describes the first lookout, Nels Bruseth, who would hurry down the trail once the sun set, hike eight miles to Darrington, and race back before sunrise.[^4] In August 1927, a flight over the forest dropped the morning newspaper outside the lookout's door.[^4] A 1938 account describes a midnight lightning storm in which the bolt struck the tower and another strike hit the flag pole, and the lookout reported fires to the Ranger Station.[^4]

According to the register, the cabin was removed for good in 1965.[^1] Firelookout.com and Willhite both say it was destroyed permanently that year, so the sources differ on whether it was removed or destroyed.[^2][^4] A 1950 USGS description called the building 14 feet square, with a cupola and white paint.[^4] The 14-foot figure is a square size; none of the sources gives the cabin's height.[^4]

The 1950 survey description places a station on the highest point of the peak, but no source says whether that marker survives.[^3] No source describes what, if anything, remains of the cabin.[^3][^4]

Willhite gives the hike as five miles one way with 5,300 feet of gain, and lists the access road as good gravel.[^4] A June 1963 newspaper said the trail was cleared to timberline, with some snow above, and that travelers could reach the lookout with care.[^3] No recent trail report turned up, so check with the Mt. Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest before you go.
