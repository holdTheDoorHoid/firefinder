Desolation Peak Lookout stands on a summit just east of Ross Lake, about six miles south of the Canadian border, deep inside North Cascades National Park.[^1] The Forest Service built the cabin in 1932, and a National Park Service inventory form credits USFS employee Oscar Banner with its construction.[^2] The National Historic Lookout Register gives 1933 instead, while the Seattle Times and firelookout.com both say 1932.[^3][^1][^4] This story uses 1932, which the inventory form also gives.[^2]

The cabin is a 14-by-14-foot L-4 design with no catwalk.[^3] The Seattle Times reports that its materials were carried in by pack horses and mules up a steep trail from Ross Lake.[^1]

Firelookout.com says the lookout is staffed in the summer.[^4] When the Seattle Times profiled fire watchman Jim Henterly, he was in his seventh consecutive season there. The article is undated in the copy used here, so that count may be out of date.[^1] As the paper describes it, his job is to watch for smoke, pinpoint where it is, and direct firefighters to it.[^1]

The best-known name tied to the lookout is Jack Kerouac, who served as its fire lookout for 63 days in summer 1956.[^5] The NPS inventory form ties Kerouac's book Desolation Angels to that summer.[^2]

The Forest Service later passed the site to the National Park Service, and the lookout now sits inside North Cascades National Park.[^3] The NPS put a new roof on the cabin in 1980.[^2] The National Historic Lookout Register registered it on May 20, 1998.[^3]

The register says the lookout remains active under the NPS.[^3] Access is by trail, and the sources disagree on the distance. The register lists a 23-mile trip from the nearest road, or 13 miles on Ross Lake plus 7 miles of trail.[^3] The Seattle Times gives a 4.7-mile trail from Ross Lake.[^1] Wikipedia gives 15 miles from the nearest road.[^5] Check with North Cascades National Park before planning around the cabin itself.
