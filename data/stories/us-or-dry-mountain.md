Dry Mountain is the southernmost lookout on the Ochoco National Forest, in Harney County, Oregon.[^1][^2] Its first lookout was a platform rather than a tower.[^3][^5] In 1929 a platform was built near the top of a yellow pine, and Jack Choate occupied it as lookout.[^3][^5] A cabin was built at the base of the site in December 1930.[^3][^5]

The steel tower came later. The Register dates the 70-foot Aermotor tower, with its 7-by-7-foot metal cab, to 1932, and most other pages agree.[^1][^2][^4][^5] Two 1931 newspaper items quoted on the Forest Lookouts history page describe the tower. One says a 75-foot tower reached the Prineville warehouse on March 17. The other gives 73 feet to the observation floor and says it might go up in May.[^3] The sources differ on the height, and neither 1931 item confirms when the tower went up.

The Register says the lookout is still staffed, and that the Bureau of Land Management has staffed it in extreme weather in 2000 and later years.[^1] The Roaming Civic's history section says the same kind of extreme-weather staffing happened in the 2000s.[^5] The sources read do not name any fire the lookout watched.

The lookout was listed on the National Historic Lookout Register on July 16, 2002.[^1] The Roaming Civic says the tower was moved to emergency use in the 1970s, and that the Forest Service listed it for decommission as of 2017.[^5]

Cheryl Hill's 2018 visit page lists the site as used for emergencies, and firelookout.com says the tower and ground cabin are available for emergencies.[^4][^2] Cheryl Hill's directory of standing Oregon lookouts, dated July 2026, lists Dry Mountain as unmanned and legally accessible (the directory says this means legal access to the site, not access to the lookout itself), though it warns that its information may be out of date.[^6] A trip report puts the gravel stretch at 12 miles, starting where NF-41 meets NF-4120, and calls the final four miles rough.[^5] The sources also disagree on who runs the site. The Register names the U.S. Forest Service, firelookout.com lists the Ochoco National Forest with the BLM, and Cheryl Hill's page says the Malheur National Forest manages it.[^1][^2][^4] Anyone planning a visit should check with the managing agency first, since the status may have changed since these pages were written.
