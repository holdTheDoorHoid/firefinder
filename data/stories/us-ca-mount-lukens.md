In September 1922 a newspaper reported a plan to make the peak, once known as Sister Elsie, a Forest Service lookout station for the fire seasons.[^1] The register dates the first structure to 1922 and says a wildfire burned it in late 1933.[^2] Angeles Adventures says a tower had stood on the peak since 1923.[^3]

Lookouts named in the newspapers include James Murray, a government lookout in 1924, and Ira W. Roberts, who reported 110 fires in July 1929.[^1] In November 1933 L. W. Elliott, who worked for the county fire department, stayed at his lookout for thirty-six hours before he had to leave it, and went back to his post the next afternoon.[^1]

The register says reports suggest that the lookout was taken apart and carried to Josephine Peak during 1935.[^2] Angeles Adventures dates the move to 1936.[^3] The register adds that photos of Josephine Peak appear to show a newer building there, and that the structure on Mount Lukens went unused.[^2] Some time afterward, the register says, the burned structure was replaced by a 10-foot-high open timber tower built to the Plan 4AR design of California's Region 5.[^2]

Angeles Adventures says a lookout stood on Josephine Peak from 1936 until the Big Tujunga (Mill) Fire destroyed it.[^3] Today only concrete footings are left, beside a small shed whose roof carries communications masts.[^3]

The Mount Lukens summit is now a communications site. A radio-site listing describes a group of antenna towers and equipment buildings there.[^4] The Forest Service describes the Mt. Lukens Communication Site as being at the top of the peak, and its project record includes a decision signed on September 19, 2012, for a new tower there.[^5] Hiking routes reach the summit, where some radio antennae stand.[^6] No source we read describes what, if anything, remains of the 1922 lookout on the summit.
