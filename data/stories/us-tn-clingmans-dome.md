The observation tower on Kuwohi, the highest summit in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, is now called the Kuwohi Observation Tower.[^1] Before that it was the Clingmans Dome Observation Tower.[^1] The Park Service says it was built in 1959 and stands 45 feet tall.[^1]

A 1958 Kingsport Times item, quoted by the Forest Lookouts site, reported that W.C. Norris Construction Co., of Waynesville, North Carolina, had the lowest bid, $57,000, for an observation tower on the dome.[^2] A photo caption on a historical marker says visitors had climbed a wooden tower before the concrete one was completed in 1960, and shows workers finishing its ramp wall in 1959.[^3]

The tower belongs to the Park Service's Mission 66 program, which ran from 1955 to 1966 and changed how parks were planned, managed and built.[^1] Just nine observation towers were built under the program.[^1] The architectural firm Bebb and Olson designed the tower.[^4]

Fire watching was part of the plan at first. The National Register nomination says Bebb's plans included an enclosed fire lookout cab, set atop the stone tower.[^4] Park Service fire staff argued against the cab, noting that while the park's fire detection system was being planned, Clingmans Dome had been ruled out as a useful site.[^4] A 1958 memo urged combining the platform with the fire lookout, but the nomination says the cab did not make it into the final design.[^4] The nomination also says the tower was meant to serve as an auxiliary fire tower, even though it is thought of as an observation tower.[^4]

We found no source that names anyone who staffed the tower or a fire it watched. The nomination says that over the past 20 years, the former observation tower was seldom used to spot fires.[^4]

The tower still stands inside the national park, and the Tennessee Land Forms list marks it as standing.[^1][^5] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on August 15, 2012.[^4] It straddles the state line, and the nomination places it in both Swain County, North Carolina, and Sevier County, Tennessee.[^4]

Visitors reach the tower on foot.[^1] The Park Service describes a one-mile round trip from the Kuwohi parking area, and calls the paved path very steep.[^1] The ramp lets visitors climb gradually to the platform, and the Park Service notes that views depend on the weather.[^1] We did not confirm current road or tower hours, so check Park Service alerts before you go.
