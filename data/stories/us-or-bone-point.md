Bone Point sits on a summit in Grant County, about 12 miles south-southwest of Ukiah, at roughly 4,500 feet.[^1][^2][^4] The tower standing there now is the fourth tower at the site.[^1]

A cupola cabin went up in the 1920s, followed in the 1930s by a 40-foot wooden tower with living quarters.[^1][^2] In April 1930 a six-mile forest telephone line was completed to the new lookout station, linking it to the Dale ranger station, according to a Maupin Times notice that the Oregon Lookouts site reproduced.[^3] Around 1947 the state bought surplus towers left over from World War II airfields, and one 30-foot steel tower was placed at Bone Point.[^3][^1][^2] A 1946 Coast and Geodetic Survey data sheet places the site in the Whitman National Forest, while the more recent sources name the Umatilla.[^3][^1][^2]

The present 30-foot all-steel live-in tower replaced that steel tower in 1961, and both the registry and firelookout.com date the current structure to that year.[^1][^2] The sources do not say why the earlier structures were replaced.[^1][^2]

Known lookout personnel are sparse. Firelookout.com names Keith Jenson in 1932 and Louise Stuart in 1954.[^2] On July 2, 1942, a minor lightning fire was reported from Bone Point.[^3] In 1954 the cab was sealed with plywood and painted inside, and rock near the top was blasted to widen the road and make it safer for supply trips.[^3] The tower was last staffed in August 2003, when it was briefly brought back during heavy lightning, even though it had not been regularly staffed for years.[^4][^1]

The sources do not say what became of the cupola cabin, the wooden tower or the 1947 steel tower.[^1][^2] A Forest Service project planned to move the radio repeater out of the lookout building and into a separate ground-level building meant to shelter the equipment. The decision was signed August 14, 2014, and the project is marked completed.[^5]

The National Historic Lookout Register added Bone Point on June 8, 2022, and says the tower remains for emergency use.[^1] The Oregon Department of Forestry administers it.[^1][^2] A 2016 blog listed the site as abandoned, which conflicts with the later registry note, so I have followed the registry.[^4] Public access and rental are not described in any source I found.[^1][^2][^4] The 2016 visitor stopped about 0.2 miles along the final road because it was too rough, walked the rest, and found the cab unfit for habitation.[^4]
