The Porcupine lookout stands on a ridge in the Porcupine Hills, about 9.7 km (6 miles) north and 9.7 km (6 miles) east of the Waldron Bridge.[^1] Alberta's wildfire register gives 2003 as the construction year of the present steel tower. It is 30 m (98 ft) tall and sits at 1,820 m (5,971 ft) above sea level.[^2]

The first tower here was made of wood and was first named Skyline Lookout, according to a 2018 hiking blog that draws on a guidebook.[^3] The same blog says a steel tower replaced it in 1970, and that the current tower is a replacement.[^3]

During a mock air attack on the night of 9 June 1977, an F-106A Delta Dart jet crashed into the ridge.[^1] Its delta wing hit the ridge at full speed and narrowly missed the lookout tower and the trailer beside it.[^1] The 1977 account puts that tower at 37 m (120 ft), while the register gives 30 m (98 ft) for the present one. The sources do not explain the gap.[^1][^2]

George Hames and his wife were in the station trailer near the tower when the crash woke them.[^1] They were evacuating the area as fire was reported all over the hilltop.[^1]

The pilot died in the crash.[^1] A ranger worked out that one of the jet's four Sidewinder air-to-air missiles had started one of the fires.[^1]

The crash site was closed off while the fires were put out, and the area stayed off limits for weeks during the investigation and cleanup.[^1] The couple went back to their duties at the lookout afterwards.[^1]

In 2010 a man who had been seven at the time of the crash tracked down the pilot's family.[^1] Three of the pilot's brothers and a nephew later visited the crash site and placed a small stone memorial there.[^1]

The province says each of its lookout sites is staffed by an observer, generally from April to October.[^4] A blogger who visited in late April 2018 was told by the observer that the season had opened almost a month later than the year before, because of snow.[^3] The lookout is also home to the observer and others, and the observer let the blogger look around.[^3]

The register still lists the tower as active and reached by road.[^2] The tower stood when the blogger visited in 2018.[^3] No source describes public visits, so treat it as a working fire-watch station, not a place to visit.
