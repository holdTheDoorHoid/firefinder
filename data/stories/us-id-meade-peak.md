Meade Peak rises to 9,935 feet.[^1] A 1942 Montpelier News Examiner item, reprinted on Forest Lookouts, called it the highest peak in southeast Idaho.[^1] That summer, with fire danger high across the Caribou National Forest, a watchman was posted on the summit from August 19, 1942, in a small pyramid-shaped silk tent that held a two-way radio and other equipment.[^1]

The watchman was named Hauck.[^1] The reprinted items give his first name as Frank in one place and Forrest in another, so this story uses his surname only.[^1] He kept a 12-hour vigil and reported fire and weather conditions by shortwave radio every 30 minutes, at a quarter past each hour and on the hour.[^1]

He lived in a tent with a stove, set among tall evergreens at the south end of the basin. The 1942 item calls the Clear Creek trail well kept, and a horseback ride up it took about three hours.[^1]

By radio, he was in contact with Delphard Nicholls of Ovid, posted on Black Mountain, and with Lawrence Skinner of Gray's Lake, at the Caribou Mountain lookout. Supervisor I.M. Varner in Pocatello could also contact each of those stations.[^1]

In a report dated September 3, 1942, Hauck relayed news of a brush fire on Gardner range land, just outside the Caribou Forest. Eddie Wuthrich, W.R. Horning and officer Ross Parker of Montpelier helped put it out.[^1] The message went from the summit to Black Mountain, on to Etna, Wyoming, and then by telephone to Ranger James Bruce.[^1]

No source describes what, if anything, remains on the summit today. A climbing report on Rocky Mountain Summits gives the approach: drive east into Georgetown Canyon from Georgetown, follow the main track up the canyon to a clearing near the end, where the writers camped.[^2]
