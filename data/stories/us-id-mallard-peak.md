Mallard Peak has had a lookout of some kind since 1914, when a watchman named Red Stewart first manned a camp here and stayed on the job for at least five years.[^3] The ground cabin that still stands today went up in 1929, and it was no ordinary building: it was one of the prototype cabs that the Forest Service used to work out the design that became the famous L-4 lookout house, later mass-produced by the thousands and shipped out in kits on mule trains.[^1] It was built by a ranger and crew from the old Pole Mountain Ranger District of the St. Joe National Forest, a district whose name, like the forest's, has since been folded into today's Clearwater and Idaho Panhandle National Forests.[^1]

The lookout sits on the boundary between those two forests, inside what is now the Mallard-Larkins Pioneer Area, a roadless stretch of divide country between the North Fork and Little North Fork of the Clearwater River.[^1][^3] A second, patrol-only lookout once stood about two and a half miles away on Martin Peak.[^3] Sources differ by a year on when staffing ended: the National Historic Lookout Register says Mallard Peak was last staffed in 1956, while other accounts give 1957 as the year it was decommissioned or abandoned.[^1][^2][^3]

After standing empty for roughly two decades, the cabin was stabilized in 1981 by former lookout Don Mathis, Ray Kresek, and a group of volunteers; Kresek reportedly stayed up there unofficially that summer.[^1][^3] Since 1992 the Inland Empire Llama Association has packed in supplies for annual upkeep, and in 2009 Tim Grubham and his family did further restoration work with Forest Service approval.[^1][^3] The lookout was added to the National Historic Lookout Register in 1996 and is also listed on the National Register of Historic Places.[^1]

Mallard Peak is not staffed and is reachable only on foot, with no vehicle access.[^3] It isn't set up as a rental, so anyone visiting should treat it as a historic structure to view and respect rather than a place to move into for the night.
