## An 80-foot steel tower that moved once and burned once

The Escoheag Hill fire tower stands in Kent County, Rhode Island, where it has watched over the state's forests on and off since the late 1930s. It's an 80-foot steel tower built to a modified Aermotor pattern, administered by the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management.[^1]

The tower did not start out here. According to the National Historic Lookout Register, it went up in 1934 on an unnamed knoll in the George Washington State Forest in Burrillville.[^1] A Valley Breeze history of Rhode Island's Civilian Conservation Corps camps tells a different story about that first site: it calls the structure the "George Washington Memorial Fire Tower" and places it in Glocester rather than Burrillville, and it doesn't tie a specific build year to that tower, only noting that the CCC's George Washington crew put up three fire towers in 1935.[^2] The two sources agree only that the tower's first home was somewhere on the George Washington forest lands before it was moved to Escoheag Hill; they disagree on which town that was, and arguably on the year as well. The move itself happened in two steps: the tower came down in 1937, and by the register's account it was back up at its present Escoheag Hill site in 1938.[^1] The sister Former Fire Lookout Sites register carries a nearby, separately numbered listing called "Escoheag Tower Site," which also puts the rebuild in 1938 and names the Civilian Conservation Corps as the builder.[^3]

The tower had at least one brush with crime early on. A fire-tower history site, summarizing a contemporary newspaper account, reports that in March 1952 forest service staff found the tower had been broken into: a stove, an electric generator, a telephone and other equipment had been carried off, and a map table had been thrown from a window and damaged.[^4] No source gathered here says who did it or whether anything was recovered.

The register's account says the tower stopped being an active fire lookout around 1990.[^1] Sometime after 2009, with the tower standing unmaintained, vandals set fire to its cab — itself a non-standard wooden replacement, not the tower's original. The blaze took out most of the upper landings and left the stairs above them damaged; scorch marks and burned debris are still visible on and around the structure.[^1] It was nominated to the National Historic Lookout Register by Jack Kelley and registered there on June 9, 2017, as US 1202, RI 8.[^1]

No source consulted here says whether the site is currently open to the public, and with the fire damage to its stairs and landings, climbing it would not be safe regardless. Anyone visiting should expect a weathered steel skeleton rather than a complete, climbable tower.
