The Forest Service put a lookout house on the summit of Mount St. Helens in 1916.[^3][^4] The last three miles to the top meant hauling the building sections up snow and ice on steel cable, with rocks balancing the load.[^4] That summer a newspaper said at least one lookout had gone up, but warned that the station would see little use if the rains kept up.[^3] The planned house was described as a timber building, twelve feet square, with a six-foot-square cupola.[^3]

The cupola took longer. Work on it started in 1918 and continued until 1921, held up by weather that was hard to predict on the summit, according to the Former Fire Lookout Sites Register.[^1] HistoryLink says the D-6 cupola took three years of effort, from 1918 to 1921.[^5]

A July 1922 newspaper said a Forest Service man lived in the house from July into early fall, scanning the landscape for fires on clear days.[^3] WillhiteWeb says Edward Robinson, a patrolman out of Spirit Lake, most likely staffed the site first, in 1916.[^4] It names George Schnitzler in 1922, Glen H. Meissner in 1924 and Kenneth Macdonald in 1926.[^3][^4]

In 1924 Meissner reported 16 fires in one day.[^3][^4] The newspaper links one of those fires to the stock show buildings in Portland, while WillhiteWeb calls it a single building there.[^3][^4] In 1919 a temporary worker named John Walker was caught in a rock slide while mending the telephone line to the lookout.[^3][^4] His injuries were fatal before he could be treated.[^3][^4]

Around 1929 the lookout fell out of use.[^1][^2][^4] HistoryLink says summit stations like this one did not work out, because their height left them open to bad weather that blocked the views.[^5]

A family account on the register page, which we could not corroborate, places a Forest Service worker on the Spirit Lake district in 1927 and says he helped take usable equipment down from the lookout during a summer assignment.[^1] WillhiteWeb reports that the Osborne fire finder broke loose from a backpack on the way down and was never found again.[^4] A contributor to the register also recalled having lunch on what was left of the wooden house in the summer of 1950.[^1]

The May 1980 eruption wiped out whatever remained.[^1] By 1980, the register says, only the foundation was left; WillhiteWeb adds a few remnants.[^1][^4]

We found no source that describes the site today, or whether visitors can reach it.
