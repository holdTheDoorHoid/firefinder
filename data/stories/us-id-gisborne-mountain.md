Gisborne Mountain Lookout stands on a summit about 10 miles northwest of Sandpoint, in Bonner County, Idaho.[^3][^1] The summit was first known as Looking Glass Mountain.[^1] The first lookout there was a 40-foot pole tower with an L-4 cab, built in 1932.[^1][^2][^4] A September 1933 Forest Service bulletin reported humidity readings taken at Looking Glass Lookout near the Priest River Experiment Station.[^4]

Newspaper items reprinted on a local history site name a few of the people who worked the lookout.[^4] Kenneth Osborn was the lookout at the Looking Glass station in 1934, and C.A. Stoner was transferred away from it in 1936.[^4] The Idaho Fire Lookouts site lists staff through 1984, including several couples.[^2]

The summit was renamed Gisborne Mountain to honor Harry T. Gisborne, who did fire research on the surrounding Priest River Experimental Forest.[^1][^2] The Idaho site gives 1949 for the rename.[^2] A July 1950 newspaper item says the national board of geographic names had just approved the new name, and that it honored Gisborne, who had died the previous November.[^4] The two dates do not line up, so both are reported here.[^2][^4]

The tower standing today went up in 1958.[^1][^3] The local history page calls it a treated-timber tower, 41 feet tall, with an R-6 cab, and the Idaho site dates the R-6 cab to 1958.[^2][^4] The National Historic Lookout Register describes a wooden tower, 41 feet tall, with a 15-by-15-foot flat-topped cab designed for living in, plus a catwalk.[^1] A July 1958 Sandpoint newspaper item places Fulton Messmore on the mountain, building a lookout for the Forest Service.[^4]

The National Historic Lookout Register added the lookout on August 1, 1995.[^1][^2] The Priest River Experimental Forest's facilities page also says the Gisborne lookout is on the register.[^5] About half a mile east-southeast, a patrol point once had a 30-foot pole platform.[^4][^2] When the Idaho site's writers walked out to it, they found only the remains.[^2]

The Idaho Fire Lookouts site describes Gisborne Mountain as drive-up and staffed in summer.[^2][^3] Staffing is seasonal, so a visit can miss the lookout.[^2] When the site's writers came by, smoke from Pacific Northwest fires made visibility very poor, the lookout had taken some time off, and they left a note at the base of the stairs.[^2] The register lists the Priest Lake Ranger District for the lookout, so a visitor planning a trip could check current access with that office.[^1]
