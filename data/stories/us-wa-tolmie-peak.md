Tolmie Peak's lookout is one of four fire towers the National Park Service built inside Mount Rainier National Park between 1932 and 1934.[^1][^2] Seven lookouts once stood inside the park in the 1930s; Tolmie Peak is one of only four that remain.[^4] It went up in 1933, designed under NPS architect Edwin A. Nickel in the agency's standard rustic two-story style: a lookout room on top with storage below.[^1][^3] Soon after it was finished, a windstorm tore the roof off, and crews had to rebuild it; cables anchored to buried "deadmen" still brace the structure against wind today.[^2]

The peak takes its name from William Fraser Tolmie, a Hudson's Bay Company employee who explored up the Puyallup River valley with Native guides in the early 1800s and is counted as the first non-Native person to set foot in what became the park.[^1]

Unlike many backcountry lookouts that were decommissioned and left to decay, Tolmie Peak's tower has stayed in occasional use. It's sometimes staffed by volunteers in summer and serves as a weekend visitor-contact point, where NPS staff or volunteers talk with hikers who make the climb.[^1][^2] It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on March 13, 1991, as part of the larger Mount Rainier National Historic Landmark District that covers the park's whole stock of Park Service rustic architecture.[^2]

The lookout sits above Eunice Lake and is reached by a popular, family-friendly trail from the Mowich Lake area, a hike of a few miles with modest elevation gain and views that stretch to Mount Rainier, the Olympics, and on clear days Mount St. Helens.[^4] Parking at the Mowich Lake trailhead can fill up in peak season. The tower shows real wear from decades of mountain weather, and visitors should treat it as a historic structure rather than a shelter to count on.[^5]
