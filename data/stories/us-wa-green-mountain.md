Green Mountain Lookout sits at the summit of Green Mountain, deep in what is now the Glacier Peak Wilderness of the Mount Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest. The Civilian Conservation Corps built it in 1933 to a standard Forest Service plan: a single-story, 14-by-14-foot wood-frame cab with an exterior catwalk and a cable system to anchor it against mountain winds.[^1] It is one of the classic "L-4" style ground houses that were once common across the Pacific Northwest.[^2]

The lookout held up to decades of severe winter weather, though not without damage. In 1950 crews replaced the roof and repaired walls, windows and doors after a bad winter's snow load the year before.[^1] It kept its fire-detection role into the 1980s, but by the mid-1990s the building had deteriorated enough — a damaged foundation and catwalk — that the Forest Service closed it to the public.[^1] Around that time the Friends of Green Mountain, one of the first volunteer groups of its kind, began a long restoration effort.[^2]

The repair work did not go smoothly. In 2002, after a newly installed foundation was itself wrecked by snowfall, the Forest Service had the whole building dismantled and flown out piece by piece for off-site repair.[^1] Road washouts delayed the project further in 2003 and 2006. The foundation was finally fixed in 2009, and the lookout was reassembled on the summit, with its pieces flown back up by helicopter.[^1]

That helicopter use became a legal flashpoint. In 2010 the group Wilderness Watch sued the Forest Service, arguing the agency had broken wilderness law by using motorized aircraft for the restoration. A federal court in Seattle agreed in 2012 and ordered the lookout removed. The ruling set off a fight in Congress: Senator Patty Murray introduced the Green Mountain Lookout Heritage Protection Act, which barred removal of the structure unless it threatened public safety. Congress passed it and President Obama signed it into law in 2014, overriding the court order.[^1] The National Trust for Historic Preservation backed the bill, and in nearby Darrington the news reportedly drew a standing ovation for the mayor.[^1]

The lookout has been listed on the National Register of Historic Places since February 1988 and on the National Historic Lookout Register since October 1991.[^1][^2] It is no longer staffed for fire detection, but thanks to the Friends of Green Mountain it still stands, restored, atop its granite summit.[^2]
