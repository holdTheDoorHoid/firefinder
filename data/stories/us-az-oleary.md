O'Leary Peak waited a long time for its lookout.[^2] A 1952 Forest Service memo says the agency had wanted one there for about thirty years, to cover the Cinder Hills and other areas that other lookouts could not see.[^2] A 1959 newspaper said the tower was meant to cover one of the existing blind spots, the Sunset Crater area.[^2] The contract went to Martin Cline of Globe, Arizona, with a winning bid of $5,480.[^2] By June 1959 his crew had erected a 30-foot tower, and the Forest Service built an $18,000 access road to serve it.[^2]

A late-July 1959 newspaper profile names Marion J. Bedwell, a Flagstaff gunsmith, as the tower's occupant from July 11, and says he and his Siberian husky, Buck, were living there.[^2] The same profile said he had already plotted 27 fires, all quickly checked.[^2]

Sources disagree on the size. The register describes a 14-by-14-foot live-in cab on a 32-foot metal tower, while the 1959 build log says 30 feet and the newspaper describes a 20-foot-square glass-enclosed tower.[^1][^2]

The register added the lookout on March 15, 2006, as US 679, nominated by Dave Lorenz.[^1] The register lists the lookout at 8,916 feet, the Forest Service trail page says the tower sits at 8,911, and Wikipedia's infobox gives 8,919 feet for the peak's highest point.[^1][^3][^4]

Today the Forest Service trail page says the tower is not on the peak itself, which it calls O'Leary's western summit. Wikipedia places the tower on a subsidiary eastern peak.[^3][^4] Hikers reach it on foot.[^3] The route starts at a blocked forest road, and from there it is about another mile to the base of the peak, then six switchbacks to the tower.[^3] An alert on that page says O'Leary access is restricted and NPS Road 545 is closed between SR 89 and Sunset Crater, so check before you go.[^3] I found no recent staffing report, so it is unclear whether anyone is on duty in the cab.
