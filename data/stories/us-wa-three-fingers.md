Three Fingers Lookout clings to a rock spire on Three Fingers Mountain, southwest of Darrington, in what's now the Boulder River Wilderness of the Mount Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest.[^1][^2] Darrington District Ranger Harold Engles and trail foreman Harry Bedal scouted the peak in 1929 as a fire-watch site, and construction followed over roughly three years, from 1930 to 1932.[^1][^2] Crews blasted 15 feet off the pinnacle's top to make a level foundation, packed materials in by horse to within 600 feet of the summit, and then hauled everything the rest of the way by a tram and windlass rigged from telephone wire, finishing the climb on ropes and ladders.[^1][^2] The finished building is a standard 14-by-14-foot L-4 cab, though it lacks the usual outside catwalk because the site's rock ledges left no room for one.[^2]

The lookout was staffed seasonally, with the National Historic Lookout Register giving 1931 to 1943 and Wikipedia's account (drawing on the NRHP nomination) giving 1933 to 1942; either way, it was abandoned after about a decade of use, reportedly for financial reasons during wartime budget cuts.[^1][^2]

The building might have been lost to decay if not for the daughter of its last observer, who led a restoration effort completed in 1986.[^1] Retired Ranger Engles, who had pushed to save the lookout, lived to see the restored building before he died in August 1993.[^1] The Everett branch of The Mountaineers, a local climbing and hiking club, has maintained it since; its Three Fingers lookout and trail committee has kept up repairs since the mid-1990s.[^1][^3] The lookout was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1987.[^2]

By 2015 the roof was leaking and overdue for an overhaul, so Mountaineers volunteers replaced it with new cedar shingles, fixed windows, and repainted the cab. A Snohomish County Sheriff's Office helicopter — normally barred from wilderness work, but allowed here after a Forest Service review — flew in the roofing materials and tools by cargo net, saving volunteers from packing them in by hand.[^3]

Reaching Three Fingers today is a serious undertaking: about 16 miles round trip with roughly 5,000 feet of elevation gain, which most hikers split over two days, and the final approach to the lookout itself involves exposed scrambling.[^3] It's not rented out; it stands as a volunteer-maintained destination for experienced hikers and climbers.
