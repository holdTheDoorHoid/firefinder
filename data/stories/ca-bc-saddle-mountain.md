Saddle Mountain stands about 12 kilometres southwest of Nakusp, near the community of Arrow Park, in British Columbia.[^1] The lookout sits on a rocky knob above the treeline.[^1]

The Forest Service selected the summit in the 1930s as a fire lookout because of its wide-ranging outlook.[^2] In November 1939 a Forest Branch newsletter item said that the station VE9ES had been picked up in Korea, and it named the station as Saddle Mountain Lookout.[^3] The register traces the site's shelter through a tent, then a log cabin, and finally a prefabricated building.[^1]

Ranger Percy Young at Nakusp had kept a lookout on the peak for many years.[^3] Eric Johansen was the lookoutman when a storm struck on Sunday night, July 6.[^3] A newsletter item dated October 20, 1941 says a bolt struck a stump about two feet from the camp tent.[^3] The lookout building was moved off its foundation and generally wrecked, and Johansen had suffered from a slight concussion.[^3]

The lookout was struck again on August 26, 1943.[^3] A December 1943 item says the tent frame was demolished, the tent partly burned, and the radio antenna and mast were destroyed.[^3] Sam Jackson, who manned the lookout, was in the tent quarters slightly below the peak and was not injured.[^3] A separate item calls the damage to the lookout negligible, so the accounts disagree.[^3]

Mainly because of labour difficulties, the December report says, the building had not been rebuilt, and tent accommodation had been in use for the past two summers.[^3] The heritage register lists the present lookout as dating from 1963, with a staff cabin from 1947 built below it.[^1]

The register says the site includes both the trail and the Saddle Mountain Fire Lookout Recreation Site, and notes that the trail still draws both visitors and residents.[^1] The register says the Nakusp and Area Community Trail Society currently maintains the trail, and it records a 2015 restoration by the Ministry of Forests, Recreation Sites and Trails.[^1]

The trail is 5.3 km long and climbs to the lookout at the summit.[^1] The lower section, hit by a major windstorm and blowdown in 2018, is not currently maintained.[^2] The trail society suggests that hikers consider starting from the upper parking lot and rejoining the trail above the blowdown.[^2] Technical climbing is not needed, although hikers who fear heights may be uncomfortable on the exposed climb to the lookout.[^2]
