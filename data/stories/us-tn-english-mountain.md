The English Mountain Fire Lookout Tower stands on Carson Springs Road near Chestnut Hill in Cocke County, Tennessee.[^1] The Tennessee Division of Forestry administers it.[^1][^3] The Civilian Conservation Corps built it in 1934, and its steel bears the marker of the Carnegie Steel Company.[^1] It is 60 feet tall with a seven-by-seven-foot cab.[^1] The nomination says it is likely an Aermotor MC-39.[^1] It also calls Aermotor, a Chicago company, the leading maker of steel fire towers in the early twentieth century.[^1]

The 1934 deed transferred the property to the State of Tennessee Forestry Service.[^1] The nomination says very little historical information is available about this tower in particular.[^1] A lookout operator's cabin from about 1934, seen in a photograph from around 1948, is no longer extant.[^1] A four-by-six-foot cabin replaced it in 1960, and the nomination says that cabin was removed sometime after 1985.[^1]

The nomination ties the tower to a state forestry program that relied heavily on CCC labor.[^1] It names no lookout operators and no fires the tower watched.[^1] The tower's period of significance runs from 1934 to 1965, and the nomination ends it when Tennessee's towers were largely abandoned.[^1]

The nomination describes the tower as in overall good condition, with normal wear and tear.[^1] Its current functions are listed as emergency fire lookout and as vacant, not in use.[^1] The nomination says the tower has not been used for observation since the mid-1970s, though forestry staff occasionally climb it for maintenance checks.[^1] The observation room's trapdoor was locked at the time of the survey, and the interior of the cab was inaccessible.[^1]

The sources disagree on elevation. The Register gives 3,617 feet, while the nomination gives 3,629 feet.[^3][^1]

The Park Service's record for the tower is dated November 20, 2015.[^2] Wikipedia, citing the Park Service, says the tower has a single access road, Carson Springs Road.[^4] A wildfire burned several hundred acres on the mountain in spring 2016, and the sources do not say whether the tower was involved.[^4] Check access with the Tennessee Division of Forestry before any visit, and do not climb a tower that is locked or closed.
