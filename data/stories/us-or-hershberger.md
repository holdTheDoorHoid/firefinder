Rogue River National Forest managers first talked about putting a lookout on Hershberger Mountain as early as 1913, when a fire-detection line was proposed to the peak as part of a district fire plan.[^6] A camp existed on the mountain by 1917, and the lookout house itself, a D-6 cupola-style cabin, was built in 1925.[^2][^3] The design was standard for the Forest Service's District Six (later renamed Region Six, the source of the "D-6" label) lookouts built in Oregon and Washington in the 1920s: a wood-frame ground cabin with a hipped, wood-shingled roof and a second-story cupola that held the fire-spotting station and an Osborne firefinder alidade.[^3][^4] Downstairs held the furnishings needed to live there, a stove, table, cupboards and a bed, with a hinged ladder in the ceiling connecting that living space to the fire-spotting post above.[^4]

During the Second World War the lookout did double duty as an aircraft warning station, part of the home-front network watching Pacific coast skies.[^5] Its official period of historic significance for fire detection on the Rogue River runs from 1925 to 1943, and the Forest Service's own records list it as vacant in more recent decades rather than staffed year-round.[^4] A second, smaller structure on the site, a privy, has been described as long abandoned and badly deteriorated.[^4]

Hershberger was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on December 29, 2000, as part of a multiple-property nomination covering Forest Service historic structures on the Rogue River National Forest.[^2][^4] It was refurbished once in the 1980s, and again in 2012, when crews gave it a new roof, shutters, glass, paint and other repairs.[^5][^6] The plank catwalk now wrapping three sides of the cupola is a later addition, replacing an earlier two-sided version built in the 1960s.[^4]

Today the lookout still sees occasional use by the Forest Service and is listed on the National Historic Lookout Register, though it has not been actively staffed in recent years.[^1][^5] It sits above the Upper Rogue River with views toward Crater Lake, Mount Thielsen, Mount Bailey, Mount McLoughlin and, on clear days, Mount Shasta.[^5] Reaching it means following Forest Roads 6510, 6515 and 530 north of Union Creek, Oregon, a rough, pothole-prone route best suited to a high-clearance vehicle, ending in a short hike to the structure.[^5]
