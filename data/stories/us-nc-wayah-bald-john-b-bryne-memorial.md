Wayah Bald is a high, treeless open area in the Nantahala National Forest, near Franklin, North Carolina, in Macon County.[^1][^2] The stone observation tower there was built by the Civilian Conservation Corps for fire detection.[^2] Wikipedia and the register date that work to 1937, but the Forest Service says the Corps built the tower in 1935.[^2][^1][^3]

A wooden lookout stood on the summit first, and the Forest Service dates it to about 1929.[^3] A 1936 newspaper report, quoted by Eastern US Lookouts, described it as a 25-foot tower that had been used for 10 years before it was razed.[^4] The same report planned a 43-foot stone tower with living quarters for the warden.[^4] According to the Forest Service, the finished tower was 53 feet tall, made of quarry stone with an oak roof on top.[^3] It was dedicated on Labor Day in 1937 as the John B. Bryne Memorial Tower, and the Forest Service says it opened to the public on September 6th, 1937.[^4][^3]

Its lookout years were short. The Forest Service says the tower went out of service in 1945.[^3] According to the register, faulty mortar was the reason its service as a fire lookout lasted only eight years.[^1] The register dates the cab's removal to 1947 and says the stone tower then became a popular viewpoint near the Appalachian Trail.[^1] The Forest Service says the tower was restored again in 1983.[^3] Wikipedia says the Forest Service restored the north face in 2009 and 2010.[^2]

The most dramatic chapter came on November 23, 2016, when the Camp Branch Fire burned the upper structure, and only the stone base was left.[^4] The Appalachian Trail Conservancy says the wooden top was burned to charcoal almost instantly.[^5] Wikipedia says the tower was shut to visitors for a time after the fire, and it reopened in 2018.[^2] Jeff Johnson Timber Frames and the Nantahala National Forest agreed to a plan in which the forest would supply the timber and the firm would design and build the new top.[^5] The Forest Service says its most recent alteration swapped out the wooden roof, which the 2016 wildfire had damaged.[^3]

Today the tower sits a few feet from the Appalachian Trail and the Bartram Trail, and visitors often stop there.[^5] The register lists the U.S. Forest Service as the administering agency.[^1] Nothing in the sources gives current climbing rules, so check with the Nantahala National Forest before going up.
