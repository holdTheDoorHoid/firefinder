In October 1941, Wasatch National Forest officials said they believed their post on a downtown office-building roof was the nation's only one of its kind.[^1]

The station sat on the Walker Bank Building, which was completed in 1912.[^2][^4] The sources disagree on its height: Intermountain Histories and Clio call it sixteen stories, while the register and Forest Lookouts say fifteen.[^2][^4][^3][^1]

The Wasatch National Forest and the Salt Lake City Water Department set up the lookout in 1939, with the bank's permission.[^2][^3] Most of the city's water came from streams in the mountains around the valley, and grazing and wildfire had damaged the watersheds that fed them. The station was there to spot fires in the foothills before they could reach the city.[^2][^3]

Nothing enclosed the station. The operator used a three-legged table with a sighting device to pinpoint fires, working beneath a three-story steel tower that advertised the bank.[^3][^2]

Jay M. Hamilton of Salt Lake City was hired to staff the station in 1941, and a Salt Lake Tribune report of that year said it would run from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m.[^1] In 1943 James Sarvis, then fifteen, took the post and later returned to it several times.[^2] According to the register, the lookout was usually staffed from late morning to nightfall.[^3] The 9-to-9 schedule matches the twelve-hour day that Intermountain Histories describes, although the register gives the hours more loosely.[^1][^2][^3]

In July 1939 the lookout spotted a blaze at block U, north of Douglas. A Forest Service truck and a CCC crew halted it after it had burned less than an acre.[^1]

The register says the lookout was used until about 1948.[^3] Intermountain Histories reports that the Wasatch National Forest has no records of the lookout being occupied after 1948.[^2] We found no source that describes what, if anything, of the 1939 station is left on the roof.

The building still stands, now known as Walker Center.[^2][^4] Intermountain Histories says it is owned and operated by Wells Fargo, and a photo caption dated to about 2015 notes a large radio and weather equipment tower on top.[^2] In the 1940s, KDLY radio added a transmitting tower to the roof, which was later turned into a lit tower that displayed color-coded forecasts.[^4] The building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in October 2006.[^4]

The building is privately owned, and no source we read describes public access to the roof.[^2]
