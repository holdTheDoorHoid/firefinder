Cascade Lookout stood on a ridge in King County, Washington, at about 3,700 feet.[^1][^2][^3] The Washington Department of Natural Resources built it in 1964.[^1][^2][^3] Willhite Web quotes a 1964 report from the same agency that calls the new tower a replacement, and says the lookout was built to replace the Snoqualmie Lookout, which sat 2,000 feet lower, and to watch over the Snoqualmie Tree Farm.[^3] It had a live-in cab under a metal roof, with a catwalk around it.[^1][^3]

The sources disagree about its height. Firelookout.com says 50 feet, the 2008 Eastern Washington chapter report says 60, and the Former Fire Lookout Sites Register and Willhite Web both say 70.[^2][^4][^1][^3]

Willhite Web names Linda Hill as the lookout in 1966 and Diana Justice in 1967.[^3] The tower was used only for emergencies in the 1970s, and Willhite Web adds that it had been abandoned by the 1980s.[^1][^2][^3]

In 2006, a Forest Fire Lookout Association visit judged the tower legs unsafe because of serious wood rot within the vertical splice plates.[^3] That same year the landowner, a timber company, asked the DNR whether it wanted the tower. Willhite Web's author, then working for the DNR, admits letting the offer slip away.[^3] The tower collapsed in 2008. A Hancock Forestry security guard had seen it from a distance while snow still blocked access, and after the snow melted he found it down.[^3][^4] The Former Fire Lookout Sites Register says it collapsed under a snow load, while Willhite Web suggests wind, snow, rot, or some mix of the three.[^1][^3]

That register added the site on March 25, 2012, as US 1231, WA 131.[^1] It lists the administrator as Washington DNR and Weyerhaeuser, but Willhite Web describes a timber company as the landowner. We could not reconcile the two accounts.[^1][^3]

In 2016, Willhite Web reports, the site was being prepared for a communication tower, with plans to remove the lookout remains.[^3] The author was unsure whether those parts were hauled away or simply moved aside.[^3] We found no source that says what is on the site now or whether any footings remain. A locked gate closes the road, and Willhite Web says permit holders can drive to the site.[^3] The 2008 chapter report says the lookout stood on private land.[^4]
