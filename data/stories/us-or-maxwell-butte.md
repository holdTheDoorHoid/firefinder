Maxwell Butte sits in the Willamette National Forest.[^1] The register says an L-4 cab was built there in 1933, and Kamstra's page gives the same year.[^2][^1] Ron Kemnow's notes put the L-4 lookout house at about 1933 as well, and quote an October 1933 newspaper item that mentions the lookout house and its trail.[^3] Earlier, Kemnow records a patrol lookout that used a compass around 1916.[^3]

A May 1934 newspaper item said a crew would start building a new road to the station.[^3] The register's panoramic photograph of the lookout is dated May 28, 1934.[^2]

A July 1939 newspaper named Floyd H. Clark as the lookout fireman.[^3] It gave the elevation as 6,225 feet, and the distance as 40 miles from the Cascadia ranger station by highway and 5 miles by trail.[^3]

In July 1962 Forest Service staff from the Detroit Ranger Station reported that vandals had destroyed the station, smashing its windows and fire-finder and chopping away its framing.[^3] A ranger said it might have to be rebuilt.[^3] At the time it was an emergency lookout, manned during extreme fire danger and after lightning storms.[^3]

The register says the cab was removed in 1965.[^2] Kamstra's page says it was destroyed that year.[^1] Kemnow's notes say the lookout was removed in 1965.[^3] The sources differ on whether it was taken down or destroyed.

Today the butte is reached on foot.[^4] The route runs 31 miles east on Highway 22 from Detroit to the Maxwell Sno-Park, then a mile up Forest Road 080 to the trailhead, which gives access to Maxwell Butte Trail #3391.[^5] The Oregon Hikers guide describes the butte as the site of a former fire lookout, with views across the Mt. Jefferson Wilderness, and lists a 9.6-mile hike with 2,500 feet of climbing.[^4] From June 15 to October 15, entering the wilderness from that trailhead requires entry permits, and the Forest Service page lists two types.[^5] We found no source that describes what remains on the summit today.
