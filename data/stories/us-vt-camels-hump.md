In 1911 a newspaper reported that Camel's Hump, the Washington County peak, would get the first fire lookout station the forest department had ever set up.[^1] The site never got a tower. A telephone line ran to the summit, and a cement table held a map of the surrounding countryside.[^1]

Through the 1911 fire season a lookout staffed the summit with little more than the cement table, a county map, a compass and a phone line.[^4] A June 1912 newspaper report says an arrangement had been made with the Waterbury Club, which kept a camp on the summit, to watch for fires.[^1] By 1913 the station ran only in very dry weather, at state expense.[^1] A state report for 1916 credits lookoutman Mr. Chamberlin with warning more than 500 people that year about leaving campfires burning.[^1]

Firelookout.org lists the site as a map table that is gone.[^2] The Forest Lookouts page ends with the word "Removed" and gives no date.[^1] No source I read says whether any part of the cement table survives.

The summit now sits inside Camel's Hump State Park, which began with a gift of 1,000 acres, including the summit, from Colonel Joseph Battell.[^5] The summit has about 10 acres of alpine tundra, and the Green Mountain Club's summit caretakers help protect it.[^3] Before the summit's natural area was set aside, the state opposed proposed communications towers and ski resorts there.[^5] From the Duxbury side, the Monroe Trail is a bit demanding, while a flat, accessible loop has parking of its own.[^5] Fires are allowed only at tent-platform rings and at designated primitive campsites.[^5]
