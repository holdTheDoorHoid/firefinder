## Zephyr Point (Zephyr Cove) Lookout

Zephyr Point stands above Lake Tahoe's southeast shore in Douglas County, Nevada, the last Forest Service lookout remaining in the state.[^1] Construction began in the winter of 1931, with road access paid for by Douglas County and $1,500 toward the building itself contributed by a local supporter, A. K. Bourne; the National Historic Lookout Register gives the finished build year as 1932.[^2][^1] The tower is built to the 4AR cab plan, one of only twelve such cabs still standing nationwide — six in California, two each in Oregon and Arizona, and one each in Idaho and Nevada.[^1] The cab is 14 feet square, rises 29 feet on its foundation, has a pyramid roof and banks of windows on the upper floor, and an exterior catwalk with a railing; inside was a bed, table, stove and sink upstairs, and bunk beds, a water heater and a bathroom downstairs.[^3]

A Forest Service ranger named Buck Wheeler oversaw the station in its early years. Jim Cain was serving as fire warden by 1941, and in 1940 the husband of a Mrs. T. C. Clifton staffed the tower for a summer.[^2] From the cab, lookouts spotted at least two fires that might otherwise have grown unchecked: a lightning strike in 1936 and a firecracker-caused blaze in 1941.[^2]

The Forest Service ran Zephyr Point until 1954, when the Nevada Division of Forestry took it over.[^3] Idaho schoolteachers Leonard and Margaret Hoff spent the summers of 1955–56 and 1958 there.[^3] The tower closed for the season in October 1964, and it went on being used — by one account the last lookout the Division of Forestry still operated in western Nevada — into the 1970s.[^2][^3]

At some point the lookout passed out of Forest Service ownership. It now sits on a 1.1-acre private parcel owned by Zephyr Cove Properties, with the Forest Service holding only a right-of-way easement for access.[^3] Starting in 1997, Pacific Bell Wireless leased the tower to mount cellular antennas, paying over $2,600 a year and investing $20,000 in upgrades; the building no longer functions as a fire lookout.[^3] That same year the structure was determined eligible for the National Register of Historic Places.[^4] The following year, on October 8, 1998, it was listed on the National Historic Lookout Register as US 278 / NV 1, nominated by Mark Swift.[^1]

In November 2013 the Forest Service's Lake Tahoe Basin Management Unit proposed dismantling the tower and trucking it to a new site atop White Hill, above Spooner Summit, to restore a public-access fire-watch view that development around the original site had closed off.[^4] Whether that move was ever carried out could not be confirmed from the sources checked for this story. Visitors should not assume the tower still stands at its original Zephyr Heights site, or that it is open to the public; it is private land, reached only by a Forest Service easement, and climbing it has not been established as safe or permitted.
