## A rare tower watching over a growing town

Ruidoso Lookout stands on a ridge in the Smokey Bear Ranger District of the Lincoln National Forest, in Lincoln County, New Mexico.[^1] The U.S. Forest Service put it up in 1940: a 30-foot steel Aermotor tower carrying a 14-by-14-foot wooden cab, reached by a steel mesh catwalk.[^1][^3] One source, Living New Deal, calls it "most likely" a Civilian Conservation Corps project built in 1933 under the New Deal, but notes that the National Register nomination form gives 1940 as the construction year — the date this record follows, since it's the more specific, document-backed figure.[^4]

The tower's Aermotor model is unusual for the region. Living New Deal, which lists it as a type MI-25, says only one other example is known in the Southwest, on the Gila National Forest.[^4] The structure has not seen major changes since it went up and keeps its original materials and layout.[^4]

One ranger's memoir ties the tower to a much bigger moment in history. Herbert Lee Traylor, who served here, wrote in his 1983 book *Tales of the Sierra Blanca: Stories of Long Ago* that he watched the first detonation of a nuclear device — the Manhattan Project's Trinity test — from the lookout.[^6] This record has no source for the claim beyond Traylor's own account, passed on through Wikipedia.

The tower was entered on the National Register of Historic Places on January 27, 1988 (reference 87002485) and on the New Mexico State Register of Cultural Properties the following March 4.[^3] It joined the National Historic Lookout Register later, on November 26, 1996, nominated by David Johnson, then the forest's archaeologist.[^1]

By the time of that 1996 nomination, the register noted that the Town of Ruidoso had grown enough in the tower's first 56 years to "nearly surround" it, and that a town employee — not a Forest Service lookout — staffed it to watch for urban and suburban fires rather than wildland ones.[^1] Wikipedia describes that watch as still ongoing.[^3]

The tower sits on Forest Service land. OpenStreetMap tags the structure as closed to public access, and nothing found here says otherwise, so it's one to admire from a distance rather than plan a climb to.[^5]
