The Neah Lookout stood on the Makah Indian Reservation, about four miles southeast of Neah Bay.[^1] Willhite, citing the Kresek lookout inventory, says a US Coast Guard tower built in 1942 to spot aircraft stood there, 80 feet tall with a 7-by-7-foot cab.[^2] Firelookout.com gives the same 1942 date and 80-foot height.[^1] The Former Fire Lookout Sites record adds a rectangular ground cabin.[^3]

Willhite says Art and Aunt Ruth Claplanhoo staffed the lookout during the war, and the Sites record says it served as an AWS site during World War II.[^2][^3]

After the Coast Guard, the state retained it as a fire lookout.[^2] The inventory says the site was abandoned in 1953 and stood unused for four years before the tower moved.[^2] Firelookout.com lists it as abandoned that year and gone.[^1]

In 1957 the tower was moved to Sooes Peak.[^2][^3] Willhite says an agreement between the Makah Tribe and Crown Zellerbach made the move happen, at a cost of $3,000 to the Tribe.[^2]

The Sites record says the tower was taken down in 1973 and lies on the ground at Sooes Peak.[^3] The Sooes Peak page says it was dropped or fell that year and remains on the summit.[^4]

At the Neah site, Willhite says much of the cabin is still there, though the stove and bed lie crushed beneath fallen walls.[^2] The Sites record, by contrast, says the walls still stand, though the roof and floor are gone, and that the stove and a couple of cots are still inside.[^3] Willhite says the footings have been moved, though a concrete stair step appears to survive.[^2] The Sites record counts four concrete tower bases on the site.[^3] Willhite also reports that a timber sale around 2010 cleared the trees at the site and opened the views.[^2]

The Makah Indian Tribe administers the site.[^3] Willhite says the route runs through the Olympic Tree Farm and is open to non-motorized recreation.[^2] Because the site lies on the reservation, ask the tribe before you go.[^1]
