Before Grand Canyon became a national park in 1919, the Forest Service had built a wooden lookout platform at Bright Angel Point on the North Rim.[^2] In 1928 the park replaced that platform with a steel tower about 75 feet tall, topped by a small observation cab.[^1][^2][^3] Lookouts lived in a cabin under the tower.[^3]

Edward Abbey, the environmentalist and novelist, spent several summers manning the tower, and the experience informed his writing.[^2] The same site also says he worked four seasons there.[^2] Intermountain Histories dates his four seasons to the late 1960s and early 1970s.[^3] The sources do not reconcile the two counts.

In 1933 Civilian Conservation Corps crews moved the tower to the park's north entrance, about fourteen miles from its original site, and it became known as the North Rim Lookout.[^1][^2][^3] According to the register, the relocated tower no longer serves fire detection.[^1] Intermountain Histories reports that Stephen Pyne's book Fire on the Rim cites Abbey as the reason the park ended its lookout tower operations, and that the park chose aerial fire spotting instead.[^3]

The moved tower was registered on April 25, 2009, as US 804, AZ 69, and the National Park Service administers it.[^1] A Grand Canyon history site calls it the North Rim Tower and includes a photo of how it appears now.[^2] Intermountain Histories says it is available to hikers.[^3] None of these sources says whether visitors can go up into the cab.

We found no source that describes what, if anything, remains at the original Bright Angel Point site. The Forest Fire Lookout Association lists that tower as gone.[^4]
