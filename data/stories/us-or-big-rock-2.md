Big Rock Lookout stands on Green Mountain in Linn County, Oregon, at about 4,500 feet.[^1][^3] According to firelookout.com, the spot was first set up as a camp in 1916, but the lookout itself was built in 1950.[^3] A carpenter named Knight and a crew of three men finished the cab on August 22 that year.[^2]

It is a standard Forest Service cab, a 14-by-14 design set nine feet off the ground on an enclosed tower, with space underneath planned as a garage and tool room.[^2] The register calls the design a BC-301, while firelookout.com calls the cab an L-4, so the sources label it differently.[^1][^3] An August 1950 item quoted by Forest Lookouts said the lookout was meant to cover the Upper Wiley Creek area and the upper Calapooya basin, about 50,000 acres.[^2]

The sources do not agree on who built it. The register says the Santiam Lumber Company built it and the Linn County Fire Patrol staffed it.[^1] The same 1950 item credits members of the Fire Patrol crew with completing it.[^2] It also said the lookout would not be manned that season, because nearby timber had to be felled first, and that watch would begin the following fire season.[^2] Firelookout.com says it was used for emergencies in the 1970s.[^3]

The original cab burned in 1951. On June 21 the Linn County Fire Patrol announced that its lookout station on Green Mountain had burned to the ground.[^2][^5] The Albany Democrat-Herald reported that sparks from the cookstove started the blaze and that a two-way radio and a fire-finder were lost.[^2] Every Lookout in Oregon says it was reconstructed in 1951.[^5] In 2020 the Holiday Farm Fire burned trees on the mountain's slopes, and the lookout came close to being destroyed but survived.[^5]

In 2007 the Oregon Chapter of the Forest Fire Lookout Association surveyed the lookout at the request of its private owners.[^4] The survey was meant to estimate what restoring it would cost, and to see whether the chapter wanted to adopt it.[^4] Members made a second trip that year to seal the cab for winter.[^4] The report does not describe a restoration.[^4]

A 2026 visit described on Every Lookout in Oregon found it unstaffed, with an empty interior apart from an old table and a chair.[^5] The lookout sits on private timber land owned by Franklin-Clarkson, and the register lists the site as private land.[^1][^5] The road is closed by a locked gate. A sign there allows non-motorized recreation access when the industrial fire precaution level is at 1 or lower, and the 2026 visitor parked at the gate and walked 5.9 miles to reach the lookout.[^5]
