Wolf Point is in Cowlitz County, Washington, about 17 miles northeast of Kelso.[^1][^2] The Longview Forest Fire Association administered the site.[^1]

The register dates the lookout to 1935, when a 30-foot pole tower and cabin went up.[^1] A passage from the Washington Forest Fire Association's 29th annual report describes the new tower as untreated Douglas fir poles set in concrete footings, with a 10-by-12 lookout house.[^3][^4] One page labels that report entry 1936, and a 1952 Longview paper says the state and the association built it in 1936.[^3] A Weyerhaeuser logging manager made a bulldozer available to build road and felled snags beside the site.[^3]

Height is also disputed. A 1942 Aircraft Warning Service description puts the tower at 40 feet, and the 1952 paper says 50 feet.[^3][^4]

The Aircraft Warning Service used the site in 1942.[^4] A listing gives activation on March 16, 1942, under the Portland Filter Center.[^3] WillhiteWeb's photo captions name Harry Cobain as the 1942 lookout, with AWS observers A.C. Lysons and R.W. Hukill.[^4] In July 1952 the Longview Daily News profiled Anna Wilkins, one of the few women on the district's roster. Her job was spotting smokes over the Mount St. Helens Tree Farm.[^3] FireLookout.com lists her for 1952 to 1953 and names Eva Ahlskog for 1953.[^2]

The sources disagree on when the site was abandoned. The register puts abandonment in the 1940s and reports the tower destroyed in the Columbus Day Storm on October 12, 1962.[^1] WillhiteWeb says it was reported abandoned in the later 1940s, though photos show it standing in 1960.[^4] The 1952 paper calls it one of the two major stations in the district, and says the warden planned to abandon it within two years and erect a new tower about five miles north.[^3] A separate Longview Daily News item, dated October 12, 1962, calls a lookout on the mountain top the Wolf Creek Lookout and says it was blown over in the Columbus Day windstorm.[^3] The name differs from Wolf Point, so linking it to this site is an inference from the storm date.[^1][^3]

Willhite's photo section labeled "Remains in 2017" says all four footings remain in their original location.[^4] The second growth was cut between 2001 and 2005, and the third growth is now near 20 feet tall.[^4] WillhiteWeb says brush and new growth are fast closing in on the site.[^4] Roads run to within feet of the site, but a legal visit needs a St. Helens Tree Farm permit.[^4] No source describes a standing structure there today.[^1][^2][^3][^4]
