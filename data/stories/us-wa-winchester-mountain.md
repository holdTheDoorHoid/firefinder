The peak's name dates to the early 1900s, when a prospector named Jack Post was reportedly toting a Winchester rifle at the time he named it.[^2] The fourteen-by-fourteen-foot L-4 ground house lookout on its summit was built in 1935, part of the wave of standardized Forest Service lookout construction from that era, and it sits above treeline within the Mount Baker Wilderness, with Mount Baker, Mount Shuksan, and the Picket Range all visible from the site.[^1][^2]

The lookout was staffed through 1966, after which the Forest Service stopped using it and it began to fall apart.[^1][^2] The Forest Service had actually earmarked it for demolition when, in 1982, Gary Haufle and the Mount Baker Club stepped in and arranged to rebuild it instead: the agency covered the materials, and club members handled all the work.[^1][^2] That arrangement saved the building, and the Mount Baker Club has kept up the maintenance since, running about two work parties a year.[^2]

The lookout was added to the National Register of Historic Places on July 14, 1987, and to the National Historic Lookout Register on September 15, 1994.[^1][^2] It's a popular hiking destination today, open to the public for day use or overnight stays on a first-come, first-served basis.[^1][^2] There are two bunks inside for anyone staying overnight, though it isn't run as a formal rental and may already be occupied when a hiker arrives.[^2]

Getting there takes real effort: steep snowfields often require an ice axe well into July, and the access road to the Twin Lakes trailhead may not be passable, which can add another two miles onto the hike.[^2] Snow keeps it effectively off-limits from early October through most of the following summer.[^2] Fires aren't allowed at the site, and because it sits in a designated Wilderness area, there are no toilet facilities; visitors are expected to pack out everything they bring in.[^2]
