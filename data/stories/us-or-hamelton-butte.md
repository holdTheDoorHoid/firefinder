Hambleton Butte rises about 36 miles east-southeast of Crater Lake, in Klamath County, to an elevation of 6,421 feet.[^1] Some listings spell it Hamelton.[^1] The Long-Bell timber company and the Klamath Forest Protective Association managed the lookout.[^2][^3]

Sources disagree on the first structure. The Klamath blog lists unidentified structures from the 1920s.[^3] The Forest Lookouts page has a 1922 entry about a Long Bell forest guard and lookout network, and says a lookout on the summit was part of it. That entry also names the lookout the Calimus Butte Lookout, which the Oregon list gives as a separate site, so the 1922 date is unconfirmed for this summit.[^2][^4] Firelookout.com records only a camp in 1932.[^1]

In 1951 the Klamath Forest Protective Association moved a lookout house here from Chase Mountain.[^2][^3] Its 8-by-8 cabin sat on a wood and stone foundation, and the living quarters were a converted bus from the Weyerhaeuser Company.[^2][^3] Firelookout.com instead gives a 10-by-10-foot cab in 1949.[^1]

On June 9, 1965, lightning set a snag afire at Deep Creek, south of Yamsay Mountain.[^2] Guards at the Hambleton Butte station spotted it around 6:15 p.m., and crews dispatched from Kings Cabin Guard Station had it out before 9 p.m.[^2] On June 19, 1968, a Herald and News item said the tower was no longer manned regularly, because other lookouts covered the area.[^2] During severe electrical storms or low visibility, though, a guard from Kings Cabin was assigned to watch for fire there.[^2]

In February 1965 the Weyerhaeuser Company gave the Klamath Forest Protective Association a permit for existing installations, and the same page counts it among four lookouts on the company's land.[^2]

Firelookout.com lists the lookout as abandoned in 1966 and gone.[^1] The Forest Lookouts page and the Klamath blog both mark it as removed, with no date.[^2][^3] The Oregon list also shows the Hamelton Butte site as gone.[^4] The survey first listed the site in 1953 as a Kings Cabin lookout house.[^2] A 1967 Coast and Geodetic Survey recovery note, quoted on the Forest Lookouts page, still describes a brown-painted wooden building 15 feet tall, with the survey point at the top center of the lookout cabin.[^2]

No source describes what remains of the site today. Nothing we found says whether the summit is open to visitors or who owns the land now. The 1965 permit suggests timber-company land at the time.[^2] Check ownership before going.
