Unity Lookout stands on the grounds of the Unity Ranger Station, in the community of Unity in Baker County, Oregon, within the Wallowa-Whitman National Forest.[^1][^3] The Civilian Conservation Corps built the tower in 1938.[^1][^2][^3] It is a 60-foot timber structure with a 6-by-6 observation cab.[^1][^2][^5] It was built as a combined fire lookout and water tower, with a wooden tank just below the cab.[^2][^3]

The sources do not fully agree on the cab. Wikipedia gives it as 7 feet square, while the Oregon lookouts site and firelookout.com give 6 by 6.[^2][^3][^5] A 1939 newspaper notice, quoted by the Oregon site, reports that a new Unity tower was completed in August 1939 at 63 feet to the floor.[^2] That points to a later finish or a taller floor than the 1938 date and 60-foot height suggest.

The 1939 notice said the tower gave a fire-watch view over the upper Burnt River Valley and welcomed visitors.[^2] In July 1962 the Record-Courier reported that the tower, an emergency lookout, was staffed on days of high fire danger.[^2] The sources I read name no notable fire at the tower.

Unity Lookout was still standing when Cheryl Hill visited in May 2018, and the National Historic Lookout Register registered it in July 2022.[^4][^1] Wikipedia says the tower has not been altered and still matches its first design.[^3] The station functioned as district headquarters until 2002, when the Unity district merged with two others and the office moved to Baker City.[^3] A full-time visitor center stayed open at the site until 2009.[^3] The compound was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1986.[^3]

Today the station is open only in summer, when it provides living space for Forest Service firefighters.[^3] The tower stands on the compound, on the south side of Main Street (U.S. 26), and a 2018 visitor found it not far from the road but hard to see through the trees.[^3][^4] Firelookout.com says the tower can be used in emergencies and appears to double as Unity's Christmas tree.[^5] No source I read says the public may climb it, so treat it as a working federal site and ask the Forest Service before going near it.
