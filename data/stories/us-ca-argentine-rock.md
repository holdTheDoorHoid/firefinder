Argentine Rock stands on Grizzly Ridge in the Plumas National Forest.[^1][^2] The national register says construction on its enclosed tower began in 1934 and that the lookout was first staffed in 1936.[^1] Newspapers from 1936 and 1937 fill in a messier picture. In September 1936 a road crew was set to resume work on the road, which had been finished to within a mile of the summit.[^2] A temporary building served as the lookout in 1937 while a new station was being built.[^2]

The register's first-staffed year is 1936, but an August 1937 paper says the summit had only just been turned into a manned lookout that year.[^1][^2] A new Forest Service station was completed in early November 1937.[^2] A few days later a high wind blew the temporary building off the rock and down the mountainside.[^2] The register and the papers also disagree on distance. The register's 2005 visit notes place the site six miles east of Quincy, while a 1937 paper puts it about 12 miles east.[^1][^2]

Glen Clark and Harry Bond were among the lookouts in the late 1930s and 1940s.[^2] In 1948 a lookout named Charles Farley found a grass fire about 50 feet from the station, started by a match that had flown out of his shirt pocket. He put it out with a bucket of water.[^2] The register says the lookout was in active service until 1980.[^1] In 1994 the Quincy/Greenville Ranger District sought public input on refurbishing the lookout, which it said was no longer used and was falling into disrepair.[^2]

The lookout was listed on the National Historic Lookout Register in September 2004.[^1] A 2005 visit report found the roof failing, the stairs missing and the cab floor poor.[^1] In 2021 an FFLA report said an MOU/MOA, a memorandum of understanding or agreement, had been signed for the lookout's rehabilitation, and that the work would start unless more fires intervened.[^3] A formal group, or subchapter, was then formed, and it was awarded a large Resource Advisory Committee grant.[^4] By 2025 the group had built new steel access stairs and stairs up to the cab, and was rebuilding the lookout itself, carrying materials up a narrow path by hand. The project was still listed as ongoing.[^5]

The 2005 visit described a good gravel road, seven miles from pavement, with a rough last half mile that two-wheel drive can manage.[^1] I found no visit notes after 2005, so the lookout's current condition is known only from the 2025 report of ongoing work, and whether the cab is open to visitors is not known. The register lists the Mt. Hough Ranger District as a cooperator, so check with that district before going up.[^1] Restoration is under way, so stay off the structure.[^5]
