The Whitechuck Bench lookout was a 20-foot pole tower with an L-4 cab, built in 1935 on a flat bench in Snohomish County, Washington.[^1][^3][^4] The site sat on Forest Service land in the Mount Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest, about eight miles southeast of Darrington.[^1][^2][^4]

George Clisby took panorama photos on July 22, 1935.[^3] Willhite says those panoramas show the lookout under construction.[^4] The register gives the elevation as 1,225 feet (373 meters), and firelookout.com agrees on the feet figure, while Willhite's notes say about 1,120 feet.[^1][^2][^4]

The lookout was mostly used to train lookouts, according to the register and firelookout.com.[^1][^2] Willhite thinks training was likely its main use.[^4] He notes that it had a near line of sight to the Darrington Ranger Station's lookout school, which had its own training tower, so trainees could take sightings between the two.[^4]

During the Second World War, the Aircraft Warning Service used the site.[^4] The AWS was a civilian service within the Army's Ground Observer Corps.[^5] Forest Lookouts gives the activation dates as April 1, 1942, and deactivation as October 16, 1943, under the Bellingham Filter Center.[^3] Willhite says AWS money paid for a 10-by-20-foot living cabin, which stayed on after the war.[^4] He also names two AWS observers, Nels Skaar and Elmer E. Linn, but does not give their dates.[^4]

In 1955 a private party bought the lookout and removed it for use as a vacation cabin.[^1][^4] Forest Lookouts says it was sold to a private party and moved.[^3] Willhite notes that the lookout was taken down rather than burned, so he expects no relics on the ground.[^4]

We found no source that describes what, if anything, remains at the site today. Willhite visited twice and could not find the exact spot.[^4] He thinks it lay in the area of a lower rock quarry, so the site may be gone altogether.[^4] Most of the bench has since regrown into second-growth forest.[^4] His directions run from Darrington south on the Mountain Loop Highway, then up FS Road #22, where a gravel pit on the left marks the likely location.[^4] We found no source that says whether the site is open to visitors, so check with the Forest Service before going.
