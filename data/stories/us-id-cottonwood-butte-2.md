Cottonwood Butte rises to 9,349 feet deep in the River of No Return Wilderness, close to the Payette National Forest border.[^1] Its lookout was a log cabin.[^2][^3]

The start date is uncertain. Writing in 1982 for a history of Salmon National Forest lookouts, Bing Young says the peak was an early 1920s lookout. He adds that it is not certain when it came into being, and that it was then on the Payette National Forest.[^1] The two lookout sites both date the cabin to the 1920s.[^2][^3]

The Idaho Fire Lookouts page lists Merril Hogaboam as staff for 1960 and 1961, from a former registry book.[^3] The same page lists Don Clark for 1987 to 1989, citing the Fire Lookout Museum, but that span begins after the 1965 destruction date the page also gives.[^3]

Firelookout.com and Idaho Fire Lookouts both say the cabin was destroyed in 1965.[^2][^3] The Forest Fire Lookout Association's Idaho list marks the site gone.[^4] Young's account points to an earlier end. He doubted the cabin survived much past the 1935 map, which still marked a lookout station there.[^1] He also wrote that Butts Point and Stoddard superseded it, at least within the Salmon National Forest.[^1] The 1965 date and Young's account do not match, and the sources do not explain the gap.[^1][^2][^3]

The peak lies inside wilderness and is reached on foot.[^1] The climbing guide says the trip takes several days of strenuous backpacking, either packing in from the Salmon River Lodge or landing at the Cold Meadows airstrip.[^1] Idaho Fire Lookouts marks the lookout as hike-in.[^3] The only summit description we found, Young's 1982 account, says little or nothing is left there.[^1] No source we read describes a standing structure today, so visitors should not expect to find a cabin.[^1][^2][^3]

Another Cottonwood Butte, in Idaho County 18 miles due south of Craigmont, had its own lookout cabin, built in 1952 for radio relay and fire detection.[^5][^6] That cabin was gone by 1994.[^6]
