In June 1933 the Cibola forest supervisor said an airways beacon tower on the Continental Divide at Oso Ridge was being made into a fire lookout station, and a cabin for the lookout men was to be built.[^2] The National Historic Lookout Register tells a different start, describing a 25-foot steel tower with a wooden cab and ground house built in 1938 as the first lookout here.[^1] The sources do not settle which structure came first.

The lookout had watchers through the 1930s and 1940s. In August 1934 a crew of 15 men began work on a forestry road of about two and a half miles from the Oso powerhouse to the lookout.[^2] That same year the fire lookout Sam Taylor witnessed a plane crash on the ridge and gave first aid to the two men.[^2] In 1939 the Forest Service placed emergency lookout guards at Oso Ridge and Mount Sedgewick.[^2] Mrs. Orby Swatzell was on the lookout in 1944, and William J. Porter was on duty in 1947.[^2]

In 1965 the first lookout was replaced with a CL-100 cab on a 10-foot base.[^1][^3] The register describes the cab as 14 by 4 feet on a concrete block base, while the 2020 state report gives 14 by 14 feet. A history page calls the base cinder block and says it is used for storage.[^1][^2][^3]

A 1988 review found the lookout too young for the National Register, and the 2020 report says it should be re-evaluated.[^3]

The New Mexico lookout index still lists the tower as standing.[^5] A 2012 photo caption names a fire lookout tower on the site, though another caption says a cement slab "is where the fire lookout tower stood in the previous images," so the record is not fully consistent.[^4] The sources read do not say whether the public can climb the tower or who staffs it now. Check with the Cibola National Forest before planning a visit.
