Spot the smoke

From a lookout you can see which way a smoke is, but not how far away it is. So the lookout turned the sight of the Osborne Firefinder onto it and read the bearing, a second lookout did the same, and where the two lines crossed was the fire. Try it with two real lookouts.

How it was done

The Osborne Firefinder

William B. Osborne Jr., a Forest Service forester in Oregon, built the first Firefinder in the early 1910s, and the Leupold-Volpel company of Portland began making it in 1913.1 It became the standard sighting table in the middle of every lookout cab: a map of the surrounding country centred on the lookout, a ring around it graduated in degrees from true north, and a pair of sights that turn on the ring.1, 2

To take a shot, the lookout turned the sights until the fine vertical hair in the far sight crossed the smoke, then read the bearing (the azimuth) off the ring. Firefinders were numbered so that the reading sat under the near sight, by the lookout's eye: the zero was put on the south side of the ring for exactly that reason.2

Cross-shots

One bearing says which way the fire is, not how far. When two or more lookouts could see the same smoke, the azimuth mattered more than anything else: the dispatcher plotted each lookout's bearing on a large map, with strings pinned at the lookouts (“string maps”), and the fire was where they crossed. A reading to the nearest half degree was close enough to send a crew.2 Where only one lookout could see a smoke, a vertical angle and a distance read off the Firefinder's map had to do.

That is why lookouts were placed so their views overlapped. In the 1930s the Forest Service photographed the view from hundreds of lookouts and drew “seen-area” maps of the detection coverage the whole lookout system gave.3 The map's What it could see button does the same with terrain data.

Reporting the fire

The lookout's report went out by telephone or radio in a set order: the station's name, the azimuth, whether the base of the smoke was in sight, the vertical angle and nearby landmarks. Then came what was burning, the smoke's colour and drift, the distance, the size and finally the location by legal description: township, range and section on the public land survey.2
